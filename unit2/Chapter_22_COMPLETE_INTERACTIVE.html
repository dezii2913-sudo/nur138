What is Hypertension (HTN)?

Hypertension is defined as persistently elevated blood pressure.

📊 Prevalence:

  • ~75 million Americans
  • ~1 billion people worldwide
  • Most common disease state globally
BP = CO × SVR
Blood Pressure = Cardiac Output × Systemic Vascular Resistance

🚨 HTN is a MAJOR RISK FACTOR for:

  • Coronary Artery Disease (CAD)
  • Cardiovascular Disease (CVD)
  • Stroke - #1 modifiable risk factor
  • Heart Failure
  • Chronic Kidney Disease
  • Myocardial Infarction (MI)
  • Peripheral Artery Disease (PAD)
  • Retinopathy and vision loss
  • Dementia and cognitive decline

💓 Key Cardiovascular Hemodynamic Concepts

1. Cardiac Output (CO)

Formula: CO = Heart Rate (HR) × Stroke Volume (SV)

  • Heart Rate: Number of beats per minute (bpm)
  • Stroke Volume: Volume of blood ejected per heartbeat
    • Normal: 70-80 mL per beat
  • Normal Cardiac Output: 5-6 L/min in adults at rest

💡 Antihypertensive drugs lower BP by reducing HR, SV, or both → reducing CO!

Formula: Pulse Pressure = Systolic BP - Diastolic BP

  • Measures the force the heart generates with each contraction
  • Normal Pulse Pressure: ~40 mmHg
  • Widened Pulse Pressure (>60 mmHg):
    • Indicates stiff arteries (atherosclerosis)
    • Aortic regurgitation
    • Hyperthyroidism
    • Increased cardiovascular risk
  • Narrowed Pulse Pressure (<25 mmHg):
    • Can indicate heart failure or shock

3. Stroke Volume (Volume of Blood Per Beat)

The amount of blood ejected from the left ventricle during each contraction

Stroke Volume is determined by THREE factors:

  1. PRELOAD:
    • Volume of blood in ventricles at END of diastole (filling phase)
    • "Stretching" of the heart muscle before contraction
    • ↑ Preload = ↑ stretch = ↑ contraction force (to a point)
    • Think: More you stretch a rubberband, harder it snaps back
  2. AFTERLOAD:
    • Resistance the heart must overcome to eject blood
    • Measured as Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR)
    • ↑ Afterload = heart works HARDER to pump blood
    • Think: Harder to push water through a small straw vs big straw
  3. CONTRACTILITY:
    • Inherent force/strength of ventricular contraction
    • Independent of preload
    • Affected by: sympathetic stimulation, medications, myocardial health

4. The "Rubberband Effect" of the Heart

⚡ NORMAL Heart Function:

The heart muscle stretches and recoils like a rubberband with each beat. This is the Frank-Starling mechanism:

  • Increased venous return → ↑ preload → ↑ stretch
  • More stretch → more forceful contraction
  • More forceful contraction → more blood ejected
  • This allows the heart to adapt to varying blood volumes

🚨 EXCESSIVE "Rubberbanding" from Chronic Hypertension:

The Problem: When BP is chronically elevated, the heart constantly works against HIGH RESISTANCE (high afterload)

The Cascade of Damage:

  1. Increased Workload: Heart must generate more force to overcome high SVR
  2. Left Ventricular Hypertrophy (LVH):
    • Heart muscle thickens (like a bodybuilder's muscle from constant work)
    • Wall of left ventricle becomes thick and stiff
    • Common in uncontrolled HTN
  3. Diastolic Dysfunction:
    • Thick, stiff muscle can't relax and fill properly
    • Reduced ventricular compliance
    • Less blood fills the ventricle during diastole
  4. Cardiomyopathy:
    • Heart muscle becomes diseased
    • Loses elasticity ("rubberband" can't snap back)
    • Pumping efficiency dramatically reduced
  5. Heart Failure:
    • Heart can no longer pump effectively
    • Fluid backs up into lungs (pulmonary edema)
    • Peripheral edema develops
    • Fatigue, shortness of breath, exercise intolerance

💡 THIS IS WHY we treat hypertension aggressively - to prevent this cascade!

5. Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR)

💡 Click to Expand: The "Tank Analogy" for Understanding SVR

BIGGER TANK = LOWER PRESSURE

Think of your circulatory system like a water tank:


🔴 Small Tank (Vasoconstriction - HIGH SVR):

  • Same amount of blood squeezed into smaller space
  • = HIGH pressure in the system
  • = Heart must work VERY HARD to pump
  • = HIGH blood pressure

🟢 Big Tank (Vasodilation - LOW SVR):

  • Same amount of blood in larger, relaxed space
  • = LOW pressure in the system
  • = Heart works EASIER to pump
  • = LOWER blood pressure

🎯 This is EXACTLY how vasodilators work - they make the "tank" bigger!

SVR is a measure of AFTERLOAD

The resistance/opposition to blood flow through the systemic circulation

🥤 The "Straw Analogy":

Little Straw (HIGH SVR):

  • Very difficult to suck liquid through a tiny straw
  • = Vasoconstricted blood vessels
  • = HIGH resistance
  • = Heart works MUCH HARDER to push blood
  • = HIGH blood pressure
  • = HIGH afterload

Big Straw (LOW SVR):

  • Easy to suck liquid through a wide straw
  • = Vasodilated blood vessels
  • = LOW resistance
  • = Heart works LESS to push blood
  • = LOWER blood pressure
  • = LOW afterload

💡 Many antihypertensive drugs work by creating the "big straw" effect through vasodilation!

🩺 Diseases Affected by & Benefiting from Hypertension Treatment

🔴 Diseases WORSENED by Hypertension:

  • Coronary Artery Disease (CAD):
    • HTN damages arterial walls → promotes atherosclerosis
    • Plaque buildup narrows coronary arteries
    • Increases risk of angina and MI
  • Heart Failure (HF):
    • Increased afterload strains the heart
    • Leads to ventricular hypertrophy → cardiomyopathy
  • Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD):
    • High pressure damages delicate glomeruli (filtering units)
    • Progressive loss of kidney function
    • Can lead to end-stage renal disease (ESRD)
  • Stroke (CVA):
    • Weakens blood vessels in brain
    • Risk of hemorrhagic stroke (rupture)
    • Risk of ischemic stroke (clot/blockage)
  • Peripheral Artery Disease (PAD):
    • Reduced blood flow to extremities
    • Leg pain with walking (claudication)
    • Increased amputation risk
  • Hypertensive Retinopathy:
    • Damage to blood vessels in the eyes
    • Can lead to vision loss and blindness
  • Aneurysms:
    • Weakening and ballooning of arterial walls
    • Risk of rupture → life-threatening hemorrhage
    • Common sites: aorta, brain, peripheral arteries
  • Vascular Dementia:
    • Chronic brain hypoperfusion
    • Multiple small strokes over time

🟢 Diseases That BENEFIT from Antihypertensive Treatment:

  • Diabetes Mellitus (DM):
    • ACE inhibitors/ARBs provide renal protection
    • Reduce proteinuria (protein in urine)
    • Slow progression of diabetic nephropathy
    • First-line agents for diabetic patients with HTN
  • Post-Myocardial Infarction (MI):
    • ACE inhibitors prevent ventricular remodeling
    • Reduce risk of heart failure post-MI
    • Beta blockers reduce mortality after MI
  • Heart Failure:
    • Reduced preload and afterload improve cardiac function
    • ACE inhibitors/ARBs + beta blockers are mainstay therapy
    • Improves symptoms and survival
  • Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH):
    • Alpha-1 blockers (doxazosin, terazosin) improve urinary flow
    • Relax smooth muscle in prostate and bladder neck
    • Reduce urinary frequency, urgency, nocturia
    • Treats both HTN and BPH simultaneously!
  • Raynaud's Disease:
    • Calcium channel blockers improve peripheral circulation
    • Reduce frequency and severity of vasospastic attacks
  • Migraine Headaches:
    • Certain CCBs (verapamil) for prevention
    • Beta blockers (propranolol, metoprolol) reduce frequency
  • Glaucoma:
    • Beta blocker eye drops (timolol) reduce intraocular pressure
    • Prevents optic nerve damage
  • Atrial Fibrillation:
    • Beta blockers and CCBs control ventricular rate
    • Prevent rapid heart rate complications
  • Hyperthyroidism:
    • Beta blockers manage sympathetic symptoms
    • Control tachycardia and palpitations

📋 Classification of Hypertension by Cause

Primary (Essential/Idiopathic) Hypertension

90% of all HTN cases

Definition: Elevated BP with NO identifiable underlying cause

Characteristics:

  • Develops gradually over many years
  • Multifactorial etiology:
    • Genetic predisposition
    • Lifestyle factors
    • Environmental influences
    • Aging
  • Requires lifelong management
  • Treatment focuses on:
    • Controlling BP to target goals
    • Preventing end-organ damage
    • Reducing cardiovascular risk

Secondary Hypertension

10% of all HTN cases

Definition: Elevated BP with an IDENTIFIABLE underlying cause

Common Causes:

  • Renal/Kidney Disease:
    • Chronic kidney disease (CKD)
    • Renal artery stenosis
    • Polycystic kidney disease
  • Endocrine Disorders:
    • Cushing's syndrome (excess cortisol)
    • Pheochromocytoma (catecholamine-secreting tumor)
    • Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome)
    • Hyperthyroidism
    • Hypothyroidism
  • Medications/Substances:
    • NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen)
    • Oral contraceptives
    • Corticosteroids
    • Decongestants (pseudoephedrine)
    • Stimulants (amphetamines, cocaine)
    • Excessive alcohol
    • Licorice (contains glycyrrhizin)
  • Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA)
  • Coarctation of the Aorta: Congenital narrowing of aorta
  • Pregnancy: Preeclampsia/eclampsia

💡 KEY POINT: Treating the underlying cause may resolve or significantly improve the hypertension!

This is why it's important to screen for secondary causes, especially in:

  • Young patients (<30 years)
  • Sudden onset severe HTN
  • Resistant hypertension (doesn't respond to 3+ medications)
  • HTN with concerning symptoms (headaches, palpitations, sweating)

🧬 Pathophysiology of Hypertension

⚖️ Risk Factors for Hypertension

❌ NON-Modifiable Risk Factors

(Cannot be changed - identify for risk stratification)

  • Age:
    • Risk increases progressively with age
    • Arterial stiffness increases over time
    • Baroreceptor sensitivity decreases
  • Sex/Gender:
    • Men at higher risk until age ~55
    • Women at higher risk after menopause (loss of protective estrogen effects)
  • Genetics/Family History:
    • First-degree relative with HTN → 2-3x increased risk
    • Multiple genes involved (polygenic)
    • Affects sodium handling, vascular tone, RAAS activity
  • Race/Ethnicity:
    • African Americans have highest prevalence in U.S.
    • Develops earlier and more severe
    • Higher rates of complications (stroke, kidney disease)
    • May respond better to CCBs and thiazide diuretics

✅ Modifiable Risk Factors

(CAN be changed through lifestyle modifications - focus interventions here!)

  • Obesity:
    • High BMI (≥30 kg/m²)
    • Abdominal obesity particularly important
      • Waist circumference: >40" (men), >35" (women)
      • Visceral fat is metabolically active
    • Weight loss of 5-10 kg can reduce SBP by 5-20 mmHg
  • Smoking/Tobacco Use:
    • Nicotine causes acute vasoconstriction
    • Damages arterial endothelium
    • Accelerates atherosclerosis
    • Increases sympathetic activity
  • Excessive Alcohol (ETOH):
    • Moderate: ≤2 drinks/day (men), ≤1 drink/day (women)
    • Excessive alcohol raises BP
    • Also contributes to weight gain, liver disease
  • Sedentary Lifestyle:
    • Lack of regular physical activity
    • Aerobic exercise 150 min/week can lower SBP by 5-8 mmHg
  • Chronic Stress:
    • Psychological stress → chronic sympathetic activation
    • Elevated cortisol levels
    • Poor coping mechanisms (overeating, alcohol, smoking)
  • Hyperlipidemia/High Cholesterol:
    • Elevated LDL cholesterol
    • Low HDL cholesterol
    • High triglycerides
    • Contributes to atherosclerosis
  • Diabetes Mellitus (DM):
    • Poorly controlled blood glucose
    • Insulin resistance promotes sodium retention
    • Damages blood vessels (endothelial dysfunction)
    • Often coexists with HTN (both part of metabolic syndrome)
  • Diet:
    • High sodium intake: >2,300 mg/day (ideally <1,500 mg/day)
    • Low potassium: Need 3,500-5,000 mg/day
    • Processed foods, fast food
    • Low fruit and vegetable intake
    • DASH diet can lower SBP by 8-14 mmHg
  • Poor Sleep:
    • Sleep deprivation (<6 hours/night)
    • Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA):
      • Intermittent hypoxia → sympathetic activation
      • Very common cause of resistant HTN
      • CPAP therapy can significantly lower BP

⚠️ Metabolic Syndrome

Definition: A cluster of metabolic abnormalities that occur together, dramatically increasing the risk of:

  • Cardiovascular disease (heart attack, stroke)
  • Type 2 diabetes
  • Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)
  • Certain cancers

🩺 Diagnostic Criteria (Need 3 or more of the following 5 criteria):

  1. Abdominal Obesity (Central Obesity):
    • Waist circumference: >40 inches (102 cm) in men
    • Waist circumference: >35 inches (88 cm) in women
    • Most important predictor
  2. Elevated Triglycerides:
    • ≥150 mg/dL
    • OR on medication for high triglycerides
  3. Low HDL Cholesterol ("Good Cholesterol"):
    • <40 mg/dL in men
    • <50 mg/dL in women
    • OR on medication to raise HDL
  4. Elevated Blood Pressure:
    • ≥130 mmHg systolic
    • OR ≥85 mmHg diastolic
    • OR on antihypertensive medication
  5. Elevated Fasting Glucose:
    • ≥100 mg/dL
    • OR on medication for elevated glucose/diabetes

🔬 Pathophysiology Connection - How Metabolic Syndrome Causes HTN:

1. Chronic Low-Grade Inflammation:

  • Visceral adipose tissue (belly fat) is metabolically active
  • Releases inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α)
  • Triggers systemic immune response
  • Inflammation damages blood vessel walls (endothelial dysfunction)

2. Insulin Resistance (Central Problem):

  • Cells don't respond properly to insulin
  • Pancreas produces MORE insulin to compensate (hyperinsulinemia)
  • High insulin levels cause:
    • ↑ Sodium retention by kidneys → ↑ blood volume → ↑ BP
    • ↑ Sympathetic nervous system activation → ↑ HR, ↑ vasoconstriction
    • Vascular smooth muscle proliferation → vessel remodeling

3. Endothelial Dysfunction:

  • Damage to inner lining of blood vessels
  • Impaired production of nitric oxide (NO)
    • NO normally causes vasodilation
    • Less NO = less vasodilation = ↑ vasoconstriction
  • ↑ Production of endothelin-1 (potent vasoconstrictor)
  • ↑ Oxidative stress

4. Dyslipidemia:

  • High triglycerides and low HDL accelerate atherosclerosis
  • Plaque formation stiffens arteries
  • Stiff arteries = ↑ systolic BP

5. RAAS Activation:

  • Adipose tissue produces angiotensinogen
  • More body fat = more RAAS activation
  • ↑ Angiotensin II → vasoconstriction and sodium retention

💡 All these factors work together synergistically to elevate blood pressure and increase CV risk!

✅ Treatment Approach for Metabolic Syndrome:

  • Weight loss: Even 5-10% reduction improves all parameters
  • DASH diet or Mediterranean diet
  • Regular physical activity: 150 min/week moderate aerobic + strength training
  • Smoking cessation
  • Limit alcohol
  • Medications as needed:
    • Antihypertensives (ACE inhibitors/ARBs preferred)
    • Statins for cholesterol
    • Metformin for glucose control/insulin resistance

🧠 Neurotransmitters & Receptors in Blood Pressure Regulation

Understanding neurotransmitter systems is CRITICAL to understanding how antihypertensive drugs work!

🧠 Acetylcholine (ACh)

PRIMARY PARASYMPATHETIC NEUROTRANSMITTER

"Rest and Digest" System

Receptors:

  • Muscarinic Receptors:
    • Located: Smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands
    • Subtypes: M1, M2, M3, M4, M5
    • M2 in heart: most important for BP regulation
  • Nicotinic Receptors:
    • Located: Autonomic ganglia, neuromuscular junctions
    • Less relevant for BP control

Effects on Cardiovascular System:

  • ↓ Heart Rate (negative chronotropic effect)
    • Slows SA node firing
    • Slows AV node conduction
  • ↓ Force of Contraction (negative inotropic effect)
    • Primarily in atria
  • Vasodilation
    • Release of nitric oxide from endothelium

NET EFFECT: LOWERS Blood Pressure

💡 Parasympathetic system is "protective" - calms the cardiovascular system

💥 Norepinephrine (NE) / Noradrenaline

PRIMARY SYMPATHETIC NEUROTRANSMITTER

"Fight or Flight" System

Receptors (Adrenergic Receptors):

1. Alpha-1 (α₁) Receptors:

  • Location: Vascular smooth muscle (arterioles, veins)
  • Activation causes:
    • VASOCONSTRICTION ⭐ KEY EFFECT
    • ↑ Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR)
    • ↑ Blood Pressure
  • 💡 Blocking α₁ receptors = vasodilation = ↓ BP (this is how alpha blockers work!)

2. Alpha-2 (α₂) Receptors:

  • Location: Central nervous system (brain), presynaptic nerve terminals
  • Function: Negative feedback mechanism
    • When activated, they INHIBIT norepinephrine release
    • Act like a "brake" on the sympathetic system
  • When α₂ activated centrally:
    • ↓ Sympathetic outflow from CNS
    • ↓ Norepinephrine release
    • ↓ Heart rate, ↓ vasoconstriction
    • ↓ Blood Pressure
  • 💡 Stimulating α₂ receptors = ↓ sympathetic activity = ↓ BP (this is how clonidine works!)

3. Beta-1 (β₁) Receptors:

  • Location: Primarily in the HEART ❤️
  • Activation causes:
    • ↑ Heart Rate (positive chronotropic effect)
    • ↑ Force of Contraction (positive inotropic effect)
    • ↑ Conduction Velocity (positive dromotropic effect)
    • ↑ Cardiac Output (HR × SV both increased)
  • Also found in: Kidneys (stimulates renin release)
  • 💡 Blocking β₁ receptors = ↓ HR, ↓ CO = ↓ BP (this is how beta blockers work!)

4. Beta-2 (β₂) Receptors:

  • Location: Lungs (bronchial smooth muscle), blood vessels in skeletal muscle, liver
  • Activation causes:
    • Bronchodilation (opens airways)
    • Vasodilation in skeletal muscle
    • Glycogenolysis (glucose release)
  • ⚠️ This is why non-selective beta blockers can cause bronchoconstriction in asthmatics!

Overall NE Effects on Cardiovascular System:

  • ↑ Heart rate (β₁)
  • ↑ Contractility (β₁)
  • ↑ Cardiac output (β₁)
  • Vasoconstriction (α₁)
  • ↑ SVR (α₁)
  • ↑ Renin release (β₁) → ↑ RAAS activation

NET EFFECT: RAISES Blood Pressure

🔄 The Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS)

⚡ The RAAS is one of the MOST IMPORTANT systems regulating blood pressure!

Multiple antihypertensive drug classes target different points in this system.

📊 Step-by-Step RAAS Cascade:

STEP 1: THE TRIGGER

  • Kidneys detect:
    • ↓ Blood pressure (decreased perfusion pressure)
    • ↓ Sodium levels (decreased sodium delivery to distal tubule)
    • Sympathetic stimulation (β₁ receptors on juxtaglomerular cells)

STEP 2: RENIN RELEASE

  • Juxtaglomerular cells in kidney release RENIN (enzyme) into bloodstream
  • Renin is NOT the active hormone - it's an enzyme that starts the cascade

STEP 3: ANGIOTENSIN I FORMATION

  • Renin acts on ANGIOTENSINOGEN (produced by liver, always circulating)
  • Renin cleaves angiotensinogen → ANGIOTENSIN I (AI)
  • AI has minimal biological activity

STEP 4: ACE CONVERSION

  • Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme (ACE) - found primarily in lungs, but also systemically
  • ACE converts Angiotensin I → ANGIOTENSIN II (AII)
  • ⭐ AII is the MAIN ACTIVE HORMONE
  • 💊 ACE INHIBITORS block this step!

STEP 5: ANGIOTENSIN II ACTIONS (MULTIPLE POWERFUL EFFECTS):

  1. DIRECT VASOCONSTRICTION (Most potent vasoconstrictor in the body!)
    • Constricts arterioles (resistance vessels)
    • ↑ Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR)
    • RAPID ↑ Blood Pressure
  2. STIMULATES ALDOSTERONE SECRETION
    • Signals adrenal cortex to release aldosterone
  3. STIMULATES ADH (VASOPRESSIN) RELEASE
    • From posterior pituitary
    • Causes water reabsorption
  4. INCREASES SYMPATHETIC ACTIVITY
    • Enhances norepinephrine release
    • Positive feedback loop
  5. PROMOTES THIRST (via hypothalamus)
  6. DIRECT SODIUM REABSORPTION in proximal tubule
  7. CARDIAC & VASCULAR REMODELING
    • Promotes fibrosis
    • Left ventricular hypertrophy
    • Contributes to heart failure progression

💊 ARBs (Angiotensin Receptor Blockers) block AII from binding to its receptors!

STEP 6: ALDOSTERONE ACTIONS

Aldosterone acts on the distal tubule and collecting duct of the kidney:

  1. ↑ SODIUM (Na+) REABSORPTION
    • Sodium is pulled back from urine into blood
    • "Where sodium goes, water follows"
  2. ↑ WATER RETENTION
    • Water follows sodium back into bloodstream
    • ↑ Blood Volume
    • ↑ Preload (more volume returning to heart)
    • ↑ Cardiac Output
    • ↑ Blood Pressure
  3. ↑ POTASSIUM (K+) EXCRETION
    • Potassium is excreted in urine
    • Can lead to hypokalemia if excessive
  4. ↑ HYDROGEN ION (H+) EXCRETION
    • Can affect acid-base balance

💊 Aldosterone antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone) block aldosterone receptors!

⚡ SUMMARY OF RAAS EFFECTS:

SHORT-TERM (Minutes to Hours):

  • Vasoconstriction → ↑ SVR → ↑ BP (Angiotensin II)

LONGER-TERM (Hours to Days):

  • Sodium & water retention → ↑ blood volume → ↑ BP (Aldosterone)

CHRONIC (Weeks to Months):

  • Cardiac remodeling, vascular remodeling, fibrosis (Angiotensin II)

💡 RAAS is meant to be a SHORT-TERM protective mechanism!

Chronic activation (as in HTN, HF, CKD) is HARMFUL and accelerates disease progression!

🧓 Age-Based Treatment Thresholds (JNC-8 Guidelines)

👴 Patients 60 Years or Older

WITHOUT Diabetes or CKD

Treatment Threshold:

  • Systolic BP ≥150 mmHg
  • OR Diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg

Target Goal:

  • <150/90 mmHg

📋 Rationale for Higher Threshold:

  • Concerns about overtreatment in elderly
  • Higher risk of:
    • Symptomatic hypotension
    • Falls and fractures
    • Reduced organ perfusion (especially brain, kidneys)
    • Orthostatic hypotension
    • Adverse medication effects
  • Balance benefit vs. risk
  • Quality of life considerations

💡 Some elderly patients may tolerate and benefit from more aggressive BP control (<140/90), but this should be individualized based on overall health, comorbidities, and patient preference.

👨‍⚕️ Patients Younger than 60 Years

WITHOUT Diabetes or CKD

Treatment Threshold:

  • Systolic BP ≥140 mmHg
  • OR Diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg

Target Goal:

  • <140/90 mmHg

📋 Rationale for Lower Threshold:

  • Younger patients have:
    • More years of exposure to elevated BP
    • Higher lifetime risk of CV events
    • Better tolerance of medications
    • More to gain from early intervention
  • Prevent long-term end-organ damage
  • Establish good BP control early

🩺 Special Populations (ANY Age)

Patients with Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) OR Diabetes Mellitus

Treatment Threshold:

  • ≥140/90 mmHg (same as younger adults)

Target Goal:

  • <140/90 mmHg

⚠️ Why Tighter Control Regardless of Age:

  • Diabetes:
    • HTN accelerates diabetic nephropathy (kidney damage)
    • HTN accelerates diabetic retinopathy (eye damage)
    • Combined DM + HTN = extremely high CV risk
    • Need to protect kidneys and eyes
  • Chronic Kidney Disease:
    • HTN is both a CAUSE and CONSEQUENCE of CKD
    • High pressure damages remaining nephrons
    • Creates vicious cycle of worsening kidney function
    • Can accelerate progression to ESRD (dialysis/transplant)

🎯 Preferred Agents for DM/CKD:

  • FIRST-LINE: ACE Inhibitors or ARBs
    • Provide renal protection
    • Reduce proteinuria
    • Slow progression of kidney disease
    • Reduce glomerular filtration pressure
    • Standard of care!
  • May add other agents (CCB, diuretic, etc.) as needed

🩺 Monitor Closely:

  • Serum Creatinine (Cr): Baseline and periodically
    • May increase slightly when starting ACE/ARB (acceptable up to 30% increase)
    • Stabilizes after 2-4 weeks usually
  • eGFR (estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate): Monitors kidney function
  • Potassium (K+): Risk of hyperkalemia with ACE/ARB
    • Check baseline, then 2-4 weeks after starting, then periodically
  • Urine protein (albumin-to-creatinine ratio): Should decrease with therapy

⚡ Key Principle: Drug therapy for hypertension must be INDIVIDUALIZED!

One size does NOT fit all. Selection based on:

  • Patient age, race, gender
  • Comorbid conditions (DM, CKD, HF, asthma, BPH, etc.)
  • Other medications patient is taking

💊 Overview of the 7 Drug Categories

Quick Reference: How Each Category Works

1️⃣ DIURETICS - "First-Line Therapy"

Mechanism: Increase urine output → Reduce blood volume → Lower BP

Types:

  • Thiazide Diuretics: Most common for HTN (HCTZ)
    • Work in distal convoluted tubule
    • Block Na+/Cl- reabsorption
    • Mild-moderate diuresis
  • Loop Diuretics: Powerful diuresis (furosemide/Lasix)
    • Work in Loop of Henle
    • Used for HF with volume overload
  • Potassium-Sparing: Weak diuretics, retain K+ (spironolactone)
    • Often combined with thiazides
    • Prevent hypokalemia

Effect: ↓ Preload → ↓ CO → ↓ Total peripheral resistance → ↓ BP

⭐ JNC-8 Recommendation: Thiazide diuretics are FIRST-LINE antihypertensives

Exception: NOT first-line in patients with kidney disease (use ACE/ARB instead)

2️⃣ ADRENERGIC DRUGS - "Block the Sympathetic System"

5 Subcategories:

  1. Centrally Acting α₂ Agonists (clonidine, methyldopa)
    • Stimulate α₂ in brain → ↓ sympathetic outflow
  2. Peripherally Acting α₁ Blockers (doxazosin, prazosin, terazosin)
    • Block α₁ receptors → vasodilation
  3. Beta Blockers (metoprolol, atenolol, propranolol)
    • Block β₁ in heart → ↓ HR, ↓ contractility
  4. Combined α₁ and β Blockers (labetalol, carvedilol)
    • Dual action: ↓ HR + vasodilation
  5. Adrenergic Neuron Blockers (rarely used now)

Common to ALL adrenergic drugs:

  • High incidence of orthostatic hypotension
  • First-dose syncope (especially α₁ blockers)
  • Sexual dysfunction
  • ⚠️ Rebound hypertension if stopped abruptly

3️⃣ VASODILATORS - "Make the Tank Bigger"

Mechanism: Directly relax smooth muscle in blood vessel walls

Result: Arteriolar/venous dilation → ↓ SVR → ↓ BP

Key Drugs:

  • Hydralazine (Apresoline):
    • Oral: routine essential HTN
    • Injectable: hypertensive emergencies
  • Minoxidil (Rogaine): Severe HTN (also causes hair growth!)
  • Nitroprusside (Nitropress): IV only, hypertensive emergencies

⚠️ Important: Vasodilators are ALWAYS used in combination with other drugs

Why?

  • Cause reflex tachycardia (body tries to compensate)
  • Cause sodium/water retention
  • Need beta blocker + diuretic to counteract these effects

4️⃣ ACE INHIBITORS - "Block the RAAS Cascade"

Recognition: All end in -PRIL

Mechanism: Inhibit ACE enzyme → Prevent AI → AII conversion

Effects:

  • ↓ Angiotensin II → ↓ vasoconstriction → ↓ SVR
  • ↓ Aldosterone → ↓ Na+/H₂O retention → ↓ blood volume
  • ↓ Preload + ↓ Afterload = ↓ BP

Bonus Effects:

  • Cardioprotective: Prevent ventricular remodeling post-MI
  • Renoprotective: Reduce proteinuria in DM, slow CKD progression

✅ First-Line for:

  • Diabetes with HTN
  • CKD with HTN
  • Heart failure
  • Post-MI

⚠️ Key Adverse Effects:

  • Dry, nonproductive cough (up to 20% of patients) - most common reason for switching to ARB
  • Angioedema (rare but LIFE-THREATENING)
  • Hyperkalemia
  • Contraindicated in pregnancy

5️⃣ ARBs (Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers) - "RAAS Blockade Without the Cough"

Recognition: All end in -SARTAN

Mechanism: Block AT₁ receptors → Prevent AII from binding

Effects: Same as ACE inhibitors (↓ vasoconstriction, ↓ aldosterone)

✅ Advantages over ACE Inhibitors:

  • NO dry cough!
  • Better tolerated overall
  • Lower risk of angioedema
  • Similar efficacy

Use when: Patient can't tolerate ACE inhibitor due to cough or angioedema

6️⃣ CALCIUM CHANNEL BLOCKERS (CCBs) - "Relax the Vessels"

Mechanism: Block calcium entry into smooth muscle → Prevent contraction → Vasodilation

Two Main Classes:

  • Dihydropyridines (DHPs): Amlodipine, nifedipine
    • Mainly affect blood vessels (vasodilation)
    • Don't affect heart rate much
    • Can cause peripheral edema, headache
  • Non-Dihydropyridines: Diltiazem, verapamil
    • Affect BOTH vessels and heart
    • ↓ Heart rate (like beta blocker effect)
    • Avoid with beta blockers (too much HR reduction)

Effect: ↓ Peripheral smooth muscle tone → ↓ SVR → ↓ BP

✅ Also used for:

  • Angina (chest pain)
  • Dysrhythmias (diltiazem, verapamil)
  • Raynaud's disease
  • Migraine prevention

7️⃣ DIRECT RENIN INHIBITORS - "Stop RAAS at the Source"

Drug: Aliskiren (Tekturna)

Mechanism: Directly inhibits renin → Prevents angiotensinogen → AI conversion

Effect: Stops RAAS cascade at the very beginning

Status: Less commonly used than ACE inhibitors or ARBs

Why?

  • More expensive
  • No proven superiority over ACE/ARBs
  • Limited long-term outcome data
  • Can't be combined with ACE/ARB (not beneficial, increases adverse effects)

🎯 Adrenergic Drugs - Detailed Breakdown

Understanding Adrenergic Drugs

✋ MEMORY ANCHOR: "Turn down fight-or-flight" - Reduce sympathetic nervous system activity

Key Concept: These drugs work by modulating the sympathetic nervous system ("fight or flight")

Remember: Sympathetic activation → ↑ HR, ↑ vasoconstriction → ↑ BP

Goal: Block or reduce sympathetic effects → ↓ BP

5 Subcategories of Adrenergic Drugs:

  1. Adrenergic neuron blockers (central and peripheral)
  2. Alpha-2 (α₂) receptor agonists (centrally acting)
  3. Alpha-1 (α₁) receptor blockers (peripherally acting)
  4. Beta (β) receptor blockers (peripherally acting)
  5. Combination alpha-1 and beta receptor blockers (peripherally acting)

1️⃣ Centrally Acting Alpha-2 Agonists

📌 Clonidine (Catapres) and Methyldopa

Mechanism of Action:

  1. Stimulate α₂-adrenergic receptors in the BRAIN (medulla)
  2. α₂ receptors are "inhibitory" - they act like a brake on the sympathetic system
  3. Decrease sympathetic outflow from CNS to heart and blood vessels
  4. Decrease norepinephrine production
  5. Also stimulate α₂ receptors in kidneys → Reduce renin activity
  6. Result: DECREASED Blood Pressure
    • ↓ Heart rate
    • ↓ Cardiac output
    • ↓ Vasoconstriction (more vasodilation)
    • ↓ RAAS activation

Indications:

  • NOT first-line for hypertension
    • High incidence of bothersome side effects
  • Used as adjunct/add-on drugs when other medications have failed
  • Used in conjunction with diuretics and other antihypertensives
  • Clonidine specific uses:
    • Management of opioid withdrawal symptoms
    • ADHD (off-label)
    • Menopausal hot flashes
  • Methyldopa specific use:
    • Hypertension in pregnancy (safe in pregnancy)

Routes of Administration:

Clonidine:

  • Oral tablets:
    • For chronic BP management
    • Typically 2-3 times daily
    • Start low: 0.1 mg BID, titrate up
  • Transdermal patch (Catapres-TTS):
    • Applied once weekly
    • Provides steady drug levels for 7 days
    • Avoids peaks and troughs

🤔 When to use PATCH over ORAL:

  • Patient has compliance/adherence issues (forgets daily meds)
  • Patient wants convenience (once weekly vs. multiple daily doses)
  • Need steady blood levels to avoid BP fluctuations
  • Patient has difficulty swallowing pills

🤔 When to use ORAL over PATCH:

  • Need dose flexibility for titration
  • Patient has skin sensitivity or reactions to patches
  • Lower cost (generics available)

    • ⚠️ Adverse Effects - HIGH INCIDENCE!

    These side effects are why these drugs are NOT first-line!

    • Orthostatic Hypotension: ⭐ MOST COMMON
      • Sudden drop in BP when standing
      • Dizziness, lightheadedness
      • Risk of falls (especially elderly)
      • Teach: Rise slowly, dangle legs before standing
    • First-Dose Syncope:
      • Fainting after first dose
      • Give first dose at bedtime
    • Fatigue and Drowsiness: Very common, dose-related
      • CNS depression effect
      • Can impair driving, work performance
    • Dry Mouth (Xerostomia):
      • Decreased saliva production
      • Can lead to dental problems
      • Teach: Sugar-free gum, frequent sips of water
    • Sedation: Can be severe
    • Constipation: ↓ GI motility
    • Depression: Can trigger or worsen existing depression
    • Sexual Dysfunction:
      • Erectile dysfunction in men
      • Decreased libido (both sexes)
      • Major reason for noncompliance
    • Bradycardia (slow heart rate)
    • Edema: Fluid retention (paradoxical)
    • Headache, Dizziness
    • Nausea
    • Sleep Disturbances: Vivid dreams, nightmares
    • Rash (especially with transdermal patch)

    🚨 CRITICAL WARNING: Rebound Hypertension

    DO NOT STOP ABRUPTLY!

    What happens if stopped suddenly:

    • Sympathetic system "rebounds" with excessive activation
    • Severe, rapid increase in BP (can exceed baseline)
    • Can occur within 12-48 hours of stopping
    • Symptoms:
      • Headache
      • Tremor
      • Anxiety, agitation
      • Rapid heart rate
      • Sweating
    • Risks:
      • Hypertensive crisis
      • Stroke
      • Myocardial infarction
      • Death

    ✅ MUST taper gradually over 2-7 days under medical supervision!

    If patient misses doses or patch falls off → monitor BP closely!

    Contraindications:

    • Severe depression or history of depression
    • Active liver disease (methyldopa can cause hepatotoxicity)
    • Sick sinus syndrome or heart block (severe bradycardia risk)
    • History of cerebrovascular disease

    ❌ Who Shouldn't Take Despite Being Otherwise Healthy?

    Patients who might want to avoid these medications due to lifestyle concerns:

    • People with jobs requiring alertness:
      • Truck drivers, bus drivers
      • Machinery operators
      • Pilots
      • Healthcare workers with demanding schedules
      • Sedation and drowsiness can be dangerous!
    • Athletes or very active individuals:
      • Fatigue significantly limits physical performance
      • Exercise intolerance
      • Can't achieve training goals
    • Patients with depression or mental health concerns:
      • Can worsen depression
      • Negative impact on quality of life
    • Patients with poor medication adherence:
      • High risk of rebound HTN if doses missed
      • Potentially life-threatening
    • Young, sexually active patients:
      • High incidence of sexual dysfunction
      • Impacts relationships and quality of life
      • Major reason for stopping medication
    • Elderly at risk for falls:
      • Orthostatic hypotension → falls → fractures
      • Sedation increases fall risk

    Drug Interactions:

    • Additive CNS Depression with:
      • Alcohol (ETOH)
      • Benzodiazepines (Xanax, Valium, Ativan, etc.)
      • Opioids (morphine, oxycodone, hydrocodone, etc.)
      • Sleep aids (zolpidem/Ambien, etc.)
      • Result: Excessive sedation, respiratory depression, risk of falls/accidents
    • Tricyclic antidepressants: May reduce antihypertensive effect
    • Beta blockers: Can increase bradycardia risk

    2️⃣ Peripherally Acting Alpha-1 Blockers

    📌 Doxazosin (Cardura), Prazosin (Minipress), Terazosin (Hytrin)

    Special Drug: Tamsulosin (Flomax)

    NOT used for BP control!

    Tamsulosin is indicated solely for Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

    Why? More selective for α₁A receptors in prostate/bladder → less effect on BP

    Mechanism of Action:

    1. Block alpha-1 (α₁) adrenergic receptors on vascular smooth muscle
    2. Prevent norepinephrine from binding to α₁ receptors
    3. α₁ activation normally causes vasoconstriction
    4. Blocking α₁ → Vasodilation of both arteries and veins
    5. Result: DECREASED Blood Pressure
      • ↓ Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR)
      • ↓ Afterload
      • ↓ Preload (venous dilation)

    💡 Additional Effect on Urinary System:

    • α₁ receptors also found in:
      • Bladder neck smooth muscle
      • Prostatic smooth muscle
      • Urethral smooth muscle
    • Blocking α₁ → Relaxation of these muscles
    • Results:
      • ↑ Urinary flow rates
      • ↓ Bladder outlet obstruction
      • ↓ Urinary frequency, urgency, nocturia
      • Improved BPH symptoms

    Indications:

    • Hypertension: Usually NOT first-line
      • Other options (ACE, ARB, CCB, diuretic) preferred
      • May be added if BP not controlled with other agents
    • ⭐ Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH): EXCELLENT CHOICE!
      • Particularly for men with BOTH HTN and BPH
      • Kills two birds with one stone!
      • Treats both conditions simultaneously
      • Improves quality of life significantly

    🎯 BPH Benefits:

    Symptoms Improved:

    • Urinary frequency (having to go often)
    • Urgency (sudden strong need to urinate)
    • Nocturia (waking up at night to urinate)
    • Hesitancy (difficulty starting stream)
    • Weak stream
    • Incomplete bladder emptying
    • Dribbling

    Symptom improvement typically seen within 1-2 weeks!

    Doxazosin (Cardura) - Most Commonly Used α₁ Blocker:

    • Reduces peripheral vascular resistance and BP by dilating both arterial and venous blood vessels
    • Once-daily dosing improves compliance
      • Extended-release formulation available
    • Well-tolerated when dose titrated properly
    • Generic available (cost-effective)

    ⚠️ Adverse Effects:

    🌙 SAFETY: First dose at BEDTIME - "Sleep through the drop!"

    🚨 FIRST-DOSE SYNCOPE - MOST COMMON & IMPORTANT!

    What it is: Profound drop in BP after the FIRST dose → Fainting

    Why it happens:

    • Sudden vasodilation
    • Body hasn't adjusted yet
    • Pooling of blood in lower extremities
    • Inadequate cerebral perfusion

    Prevention Strategies:

    • START LOW, GO SLOW: Begin with lowest dose
      • Doxazosin: Start 1 mg at bedtime
      • Prazosin: Start 1 mg at bedtime
      • Terazosin: Start 1 mg at bedtime
    • Give FIRST DOSE AT BEDTIME
      • Patient is lying down
      • If syncope occurs, patient is already in bed
      • Reduces injury risk
    • Patient Education:
      • Sit or lie down for 3-4 hours after first dose
      • Don't drive or operate machinery
      • Avoid alcohol with first dose
    • Titrate gradually: Increase dose every 1-2 weeks as tolerated

    ⚠️ First-dose effect can recur with dose increases or after interruption of therapy!

    Other Common Adverse Effects:

    • Orthostatic Hypotension: Very high incidence
      • Drop in BP when moving from lying/sitting → standing
      • Dizziness, lightheadedness
      • Blurred vision
      • Weakness
      • Teach: Rise slowly, sit on edge of bed first, dangle legs
    • Dizziness and Lightheadedness: Even without postural changes
    • Headache: Especially initially
    • Drowsiness/Fatigue: Dose-related
    • Palpitations: Reflex tachycardia
    • Nasal Congestion: α₁ blockade in nasal vessels
    • Peripheral Edema: Ankle swelling (less common)
    • Sexual Dysfunction:
      • Can occur but LESS common than with other antihypertensives
      • Some men report improved erectile function (improved blood flow)

    Contraindications:

    • Known drug allergy/hypersensitivity
    • Severe hepatic impairment (doxazosin metabolized by liver)

    Use with Caution:

    • Patients on PDE-5 inhibitors (sildenafil/Viagra, tadalafil/Cialis)
      • Both cause vasodilation
      • Additive hypotensive effect
      • Can cause severe drop in BP
    • Elderly patients (fall risk)
    • Patients with history of syncope

    ❌ Who Might Not Want to Take Despite Overall Health?

    • Elderly patients at high risk for falls:
      • Orthostatic hypotension significantly increases fall risk
      • Falls → fractures → loss of independence
      • Especially concerning if living alone
    • Patients with jobs requiring frequent position changes:
      • Teachers (standing/sitting frequently)
      • Retail workers
      • Healthcare workers
      • Orthostatic symptoms can be limiting
    • Patients prone to fainting or dizziness:
      • History of vasovagal syncope
      • May be more sensitive to BP drops
    • Patients on multiple BP medications:
      • Additive hypotensive effects
      • Higher risk of symptomatic hypotension

    3️⃣ Beta Blockers (β-Blockers)

    🫀 MEMORY: Beta blockers → "BEAT slower, BEAT softer" (↓ HR, ↓ contractility)
    📌 Propranolol, Metoprolol, Atenolol, Nebivolol (Bystolic)

    Mechanism of Action:

    1. Block beta-1 (β₁) receptors in the HEART
    2. β₁ activation normally:
      • Increases heart rate (chronotropic effect)
      • Increases force of contraction (inotropic effect)
      • Increases conduction velocity (dromotropic effect)
    3. By blocking β₁ receptors:
      • ↓ Heart Rate (negative chronotropic)
      • ↓ Force of Contraction (negative inotropic)
      • ↓ Cardiac Output (CO = HR × SV, both reduced)
    4. ↓ Renin secretion from kidneys (β₁ receptors on JG cells)
    5. Long-term use → ↓ Peripheral Vascular Resistance
      • Mechanism not fully understood
      • May involve vascular remodeling
      • ↓ SVR in arteries that are "being worked, used, or fixed"
    6. Result: DECREASED Blood Pressure

    💡 Understanding "Reduced Peripheral Vascular Resistance":

    Peripheral Vascular Resistance = resistance in the arteries that are:

    • "Being worked": Arteries supplying actively working muscles/organs
    • "Being used": Arteries currently perfusing tissues with blood
    • "Being fixed": Arteries undergoing remodeling or repair (e.g., after MI)

    Beta blockers reduce the workload these arteries must handle by lowering the force and rate of blood being pumped through them.

    • Bradycardia: Slow heart rate (<60 bpm)
      • Expected effect, but excessive bradycardia is concerning
      • Monitor HR regularly
      • Hold dose if HR <50-55 bpm (check facility protocol)
    • Fatigue and Weakness: Very common, dose-related
      • ↓ Cardiac output → less blood flow to muscles
      • Can significantly impact quality of life
    • Dizziness and Lightheadedness: Especially when starting
    • Depression: CNS effect
      • Can trigger new depression or worsen existing
      • More common with lipophilic beta blockers (propranolol)
    • Sexual Dysfunction: Common
      • Erectile dysfunction in men
      • Decreased libido (both sexes)
      • Major reason for non-adherence
    • Cold Extremities: Hands and feet feel cold
      • Poor peripheral circulation
      • β₂ blockade prevents vasodilation
    • Sleep Disturbances:
      • Insomnia
      • Vivid dreams, nightmares (especially propranolol)
    • Bronchospasm: ⚠️ Especially with non-selective beta blockers
      • β₂ blockade in lungs → bronchoconstriction
      • Can be life-threatening in asthmatics
    • Masked Hypoglycemia: ⚠️ Important in diabetics!
      • Hypoglycemia normally causes tachycardia, tremor, sweating
      • Beta blockers block these warning signs
      • Patient may not realize blood sugar is low until severely hypoglycemic
      • Teach diabetics: Check blood sugar regularly, don't rely on symptoms alone
    • Weight Gain: Average 2-3 kg
      • ↓ Metabolism
      • ↓ Physical activity tolerance
    • Worsening of Peripheral Vascular Disease: ↓ Peripheral blood flow
    • AV Block: Slowed conduction through AV node

      • β₂ receptors in bronchi normally cause bronchodilation
      • Blocking β₂ → bronchoconstriction
      • Can trigger severe asthma attack or respiratory failure
      • Even cardioselective beta blockers should be avoided!
    • Severe Bradycardia: HR already <50 bpm
    • Heart Block: Second or third-degree AV block (without pacemaker)
    • Cardiogenic Shock: Severely depressed cardiac function
    • Acute/Decompensated Heart Failure:
      • Can use in CHRONIC, STABLE HF
      • NOT in acute exacerbation
    • Severe Peripheral Artery Disease: Can worsen claudication
    • Prinzmetal's Angina: Vasospastic angina (can worsen)

      • Causes fatigue and exercise intolerance
      • Can't perform at usual level
      • This is a MAJOR lifestyle limitation!
    • Young, sexually active males:
      • High incidence of erectile dysfunction
      • Decreased libido
      • Impacts relationships and quality of life
    • Patients with depression or mental health concerns:
      • Can trigger or worsen depression
      • Sleep disturbances affect mood
      • Fatigue can mimic/worsen depressive symptoms
    • People with baseline low heart rate:
      • Well-trained athletes often have resting HR 50-60 bpm
      • Beta blockers could cause excessive bradycardia
    • Patients who value high energy levels:
      • Parents of young children
      • People with demanding jobs
      • Fatigue can be very limiting

    What happens with abrupt discontinuation:

    • Rebound Hypertension: BP shoots up, often higher than baseline
    • Rebound Tachycardia: Heart rate increases dramatically
    • Angina: Chest pain, even in patients who didn't have angina before
    • Myocardial Infarction (Heart Attack): Especially in patients with CAD
    • Sudden Cardiac Death: Documented in patients with coronary artery disease
    • Arrhythmias: Abnormal heart rhythms

    Why does this happen?

    • Body upregulates (increases) β receptors during chronic beta blocker use
    • This is called "receptor upregulation" or "supersensitivity"
    • When drug suddenly stopped → excessive receptors + no blockade
    • Sympathetic system goes into overdrive
    • Exaggerated response to catecholamines

    ✅ MUST TAPER GRADUALLY:

    • Reduce dose by 50% for 3-4 days
    • Then reduce by another 50% for 3-4 days
    • Then stop
    • Total taper: 1-2 weeks minimum
    • Longer taper (3-4 weeks) in high-risk patients (CAD, recent MI)

    ⚠️ Educate patients: NEVER run out of medication!

    If patient misses doses or runs out → monitor closely, restart as soon as possible

    4️⃣ Dual-Action Alpha-1 and Beta Receptor Blockers

    📌 Labetalol and Carvedilol (Coreg)

    Mechanism of Action - DUAL BENEFIT:

    Two mechanisms working together:

    1. Beta-1 (β₁) Receptor Blockade:
      • ↓ Heart rate (negative chronotropic)
      • ↓ Force of contraction (negative inotropic)
      • ↓ Cardiac output
    2. Alpha-1 (α₁) Receptor Blockade:
      • Vasodilation of arteries and veins
      • ↓ Systemic vascular resistance (SVR)
      • ↓ Afterload

    Combined Effect: ↓ HR + ↓ SVR = More effective BP reduction!

    💡 Best of both worlds: Heart rate control PLUS vasodilation

    ⭐ Carvedilol (Coreg) - Widely Used and Well Tolerated

    Indications:

    • Hypertension
    • ⭐ Mild to Moderate Heart Failure: Used in conjunction with:
      • Digoxin (cardiac glycoside) - increases contractility
      • Diuretics - reduce fluid overload
      • ACE inhibitors - reduce afterload/preload, prevent remodeling
    • Post-MI: Reduces mortality
    • Left ventricular dysfunction

    💡 Why This Combination Therapy for Heart Failure?

    Understanding the rationale - each drug addresses a different problem:

    • Digoxin:
      • Increases force of contraction (positive inotrope)
      • Helps heart pump more effectively
      • Improves symptoms (less SOB, less fatigue)
    • Diuretics:
      • Reduce fluid overload
      • ↓ Preload (less volume returning to heart)
      • Decreases workload on heart
      • Reduces pulmonary/peripheral edema
    • ACE Inhibitors:
      • Reduce afterload (resistance heart pumps against)
      • Prevent ventricular remodeling
      • Improve long-term outcomes
    • Carvedilol:
      • Protects heart from excessive sympathetic stimulation
      • Prevents arrhythmias
      • Reduces cardiac workload
      • Antioxidant effects
      • Improves survival

    💡 Each medication addresses a different aspect of HF pathophysiology - that's why they work synergistically together!

    Contraindications:

    • Known drug allergy
    • Cardiogenic shock
    • Severe bradycardia or heart failure (decompensated)
    • Bronchospastic conditions such as asthma
    • Cardiac conduction problems (heart block)
    • Severe hepatic impairment

    ⚠️ Adverse Effects:

    • Similar to beta blockers:
      • Bradycardia
      • Fatigue
      • Dizziness
      • Sexual dysfunction
    • PLUS from α₁ blockade:
      • Orthostatic hypotension
      • First-dose syncope (less common than pure α₁ blockers)
    • Weight gain
    • Peripheral edema

    ⚠️ Must taper when discontinuing - same rebound risk as beta blockers!

    Dosing:

    • Carvedilol:
      • Start: 3.125 mg BID
      • Titrate slowly every 2 weeks
      • Target: 25-50 mg BID
      • Take with food to slow absorption and ↓ orthostatic hypotension
    • Labetalol:
      • Available in oral and IV formulations
      • IV: Used for hypertensive emergencies, especially in pregnancy
      • Oral: 100-400 mg BID

    💉 ACE Inhibitors & ARBs - Detailed Guide

    ACE Inhibitors (Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors)

    🛑 MEMORY ANCHOR: "Stop the squeeze + stop the salt/water hold" - Block angiotensin II formation

    ✅ How to Recognize ACE Inhibitors:

    ALL ACE inhibitors end in "-PRIL"

    Currently 10 ACE Inhibitors available

    Often used as FIRST-LINE drugs for Heart Failure and Hypertension

    Large group of SAFE and EFFECTIVE drugs

    📋 Complete List of ACE Inhibitors:

    • 1. Captopril (Capoten)
    • 2. Benazepril (Lotensin)
    • 3. Enalapril (Vasotec)
    • 4. Fosinopril (Monopril)
    • 5. Lisinopril (Prinivil, Zestril)
    • 6. Perindopril (Aceon)
    • 7. Quinapril (Accupril)
    • 8. Ramipril (Altace)
    • 9. Trandolapril (Mavik)
    • 10. Moexipril (Univasc)

    Mechanism of Action - How ACE Inhibitors Work:

    Step-by-Step Process:

    1. Inhibit ACE (Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme)
      • ACE is found primarily in lungs, but also throughout body
    2. Prevent conversion: Angiotensin I → Angiotensin II
      • Normally: Angiotensin I (inactive) + ACE → Angiotensin II (active)
      • With ACE inhibitor: This conversion is BLOCKED
    3. Result: ↓ Angiotensin II levels
    4. Effects of ↓ Angiotensin II:
      • ↓ Vasoconstriction: AII is potent vasoconstrictor
        • Less AII = MORE vasodilation
        • ↓ SVR (Systemic Vascular Resistance)
      • ↓ Aldosterone secretion: AII normally stimulates aldosterone release
        • Less aldosterone = LESS Na+/H₂O reabsorption
        • More diuresis (urine output)
        • ↓ Blood volume
      • ↓ ADH (vasopressin) release: Less water retention
      • ↓ Sympathetic activity: Less catecholamine release
    5. BONUS: ↑ Bradykinin levels
      • ACE also breaks down bradykinin
      • ACE inhibitor → bradykinin accumulates
      • Bradykinin causes: vasodilation (good!), cough (bad!)
      • This explains the dry cough side effect!
    ACE Inhibitors Reduce BP by Decreasing SVR
    "Big Straw vs. Little Straw" Analogy
    Vasodilation = Bigger vessels = Less resistance = Easier for heart to pump

    🥤 Refresher: The "Straw Analogy" for SVR:

    Systemic Vascular Resistance (SVR) = measure of AFTERLOAD and relates to PRELOAD

    Little Straw (HIGH SVR - Before ACE Inhibitor):

    • Very hard to suck liquid through tiny straw
    • = Vasoconstricted blood vessels (high Angiotensin II)
    • = HIGH resistance
    • = Heart works MUCH HARDER to push blood through
    • = HIGH blood pressure, HIGH afterload

    Big Straw (LOW SVR - With ACE Inhibitor):

    • Easy to suck liquid through wide straw
    • = Vasodilated blood vessels (low Angiotensin II)
    • = LOW resistance
    • = Heart works LESS to push blood through
    • = LOWER blood pressure, LOW afterload

    💡 ACE inhibitors create the "Big Straw" effect by preventing vasoconstriction!

    Primary Effects of ACE Inhibitors:

    📍 Cardiovascular Effects:

    • ↓ Blood Pressure:
      • Reduce BP by decreasing SVR (vasodilation)
      • Both systolic and diastolic BP reduced
    • Improved Heart Failure Outcomes:
      • ↓ Preload (less volume returning to heart)
      • ↓ Afterload (less resistance to pump against)
      • Heart works more efficiently with less strain
    • Post-MI Protection:
      • Prevent ventricular remodeling
      • Maintain normal heart shape and function

    💧 Renal Effects:

    • Prevent sodium and water reabsorption by inhibiting aldosterone secretion
    • Diuresis:
      • Increases urine output
      • Decreases blood volume
      • Decreases venous return to heart
    • Decreases Preload:
      • Preload = Left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV)
      • Amount of blood in ventricle just before contraction
      • Less preload = less stretch on heart
    • Decreases work required of the heart

    🛡️ Cardioprotective Effects:

    Why ACE inhibitors are so valuable in cardiovascular disease:

    • Decrease SVR (measure of AFTERLOAD) and PRELOAD
      • Reduces workload on heart from both directions
    • Prevent Ventricular Remodeling:
      • After MI, heart can undergo maladaptive changes
      • Left Ventricular Hypertrophy (LVH): Heart muscle thickens abnormally
      • Ventricle becomes dilated and less efficient
      • ACE inhibitors PREVENT this process!
    • Decrease Morbidity and Mortality in HF patients:
      • Improve symptoms (less SOB, less fatigue, better exercise tolerance)
      • Reduce hospitalizations
      • Prolong survival
      • Improve quality of life
    • ⭐ Drugs of CHOICE for hypertensive patients with Heart Failure

    🧪 Renal Protective Effects (Especially Important in Diabetes!):

    ACE inhibitors reduce glomerular filtration pressure

    • ⭐ Cardiovascular drugs of CHOICE for patients with Diabetes
      • Even if BP is normal!
      • Started for renal protection alone
    • Reduce Proteinuria:
      • Proteinuria = protein in urine
      • Sign of kidney damage (glomeruli are "leaking")
      • ACE inhibitors reduce protein excretion
    • Standard therapy for diabetic patients to prevent progression of diabetic nephropathy
      • Diabetic nephropathy = kidney damage from diabetes
      • Leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in U.S.
      • ACE inhibitors SLOW progression
      • Can delay or prevent need for dialysis

    🩺 Understanding Kidney Function Markers:

    Important to monitor when patient is on ACE inhibitors:

    • GFR (Glomerular Filtration Rate):
      • Measures how well kidneys filter blood
      • Normal: 90-120 mL/min/1.73m²
      • <60: Chronic kidney disease (CKD)
      • <15: Kidney failure/ESRD
      • ACE inhibitors may cause small initial ↓ in GFR (acceptable up to 30%)
    • BUN (Blood Urea Nitrogen):
      • Waste product from protein breakdown
      • Normal: 7-20 mg/dL
      • Elevated BUN suggests kidney not filtering properly
    • Creatinine (Cr):
      • Waste product from muscle metabolism
      • Normal: 0.6-1.2 mg/dL
      • More specific for kidney function than BUN
      • ⚠️ ACE inhibitors can cause renal impairment → monitor serum creatinine!

    📊 Monitoring Schedule:

    • Baseline: Check GFR, BUN, Cr, K+ before starting ACE inhibitor
    • 2-4 weeks after starting: Recheck to assess changes
    • After dose increases: Recheck 1-2 weeks later
    • Periodically during therapy: Every 6-12 months if stable

    Indications for ACE Inhibitors:

    • 1. Hypertension
    • 2. Heart Failure (alone or in combination with diuretics or other drugs)
      • Cornerstone of HF therapy
      • Improve symptoms and survival
    • 3. Slow progression of Left Ventricular Hypertrophy after MI (cardioprotective)
      • Start within 24 hours of MI
      • Continue indefinitely
    • 4. Renal protective effects in patients with Diabetes
      • Slow progression of diabetic nephropathy
      • Reduce proteinuria
    • 5. Chronic Kidney Disease (with proteinuria)

    May be combined with:

    • Thiazide diuretic: Synergistic BP-lowering effect
    • Calcium Channel Blocker (CCB): Complementary mechanisms

    💊 Many combination pills available (e.g., lisinopril/HCTZ)

    ⚠️ Adverse Effects of ACE Inhibitors:

    • Fatigue: Common, dose-related
    • Dizziness: Especially when starting or increasing dose
    • Headache: Usually transient
    • Mood Changes: Rarely, can affect mood
    • Impaired Taste (Dysgeusia):
      • Metallic taste
      • Loss of taste sensation
      • Usually improves over time

    ⚠️ Possible HYPERKALEMIA - Monitor Potassium Levels!

    Why does this happen?

    • Aldosterone normally promotes K+ excretion in urine
    • ACE inhibitors → ↓ Aldosterone → ↓ K+ excretion
    • Potassium accumulates in blood

    Risk Factors for Hyperkalemia:

    • Taking K+ supplements
    • Taking K+-sparing diuretics (spironolactone, triamterene, amiloride)
    • Chronic kidney disease (CKD)
    • Diabetes
    • NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen)
    • Advanced age

    ⚠️ Symptoms of Hyperkalemia (K+ >5.5 mEq/L):

    • Muscle weakness
    • Fatigue
    • Paresthesias (tingling)
    • Palpitations
    • ⚠️ Cardiac arrhythmias (can be life-threatening!)
    • ECG changes: Tall peaked T waves, widened QRS

    ✅ Monitor serum potassium regularly!

    🚨 DRY, NONPRODUCTIVE COUGH - Very Common!

    Occurs in up to 20% of patients

    Characteristics:

    • Persistent, irritating cough
    • DRY (no mucus production)
    • Worse at night or when lying down
    • Can significantly impact quality of life
    • Not dangerous, just annoying

    Why does it happen?

    • ACE also breaks down bradykinin
    • ACE inhibitor → bradykinin accumulates
    • Bradykinin irritates airways → cough reflex

    Management:

    • Reverses when therapy is stopped (within days to weeks)
    • Does NOT improve with cough suppressants
    • 💊 Main reason patients switch to ARBs!
      • ARBs don't affect bradykinin
      • ARBs do NOT cause cough
      • Same benefits, no cough!

    🚨 ANGIOEDEMA - RARE but POTENTIALLY FATAL!

    MEDICAL EMERGENCY

    What is it?

    • Rapid swelling of deep skin layers
    • Caused by bradykinin accumulation and histamine release
    • Most dangerous when involves airway!

    Symptoms:

    • Swelling of:
      • Face (especially lips and periorbital)
      • Tongue
      • Throat/Larynx ← LIFE-THREATENING!
      • Sometimes: hands, feet, genitals, intestines
    • Difficulty swallowing (dysphagia)
    • Difficulty breathing (dyspnea)
    • Hoarseness, voice changes
    • Stridor (high-pitched breathing sound)

    Timing:

    • Can occur at ANY time during therapy
    • Most common: Within first week to month
    • Can occur after YEARS of uneventful use

    Risk Factors:

    • African American patients (3-4x higher risk)
    • History of angioedema from any cause
    • Smoking
    • Seasonal allergies

    🚨 EMERGENCY MANAGEMENT:

    • STOP ACE inhibitor IMMEDIATELY!
    • Call 911 / Seek emergency care
    • Maintain airway (may need intubation)
    • Treatment:
      • Epinephrine (if severe/airway involvement)
      • Antihistamines (diphenhydramine)
      • Corticosteroids
      • Fresh frozen plasma (for bradykinin-mediated angioedema)

    ⚠️ NEVER rechallenge with ANY ACE inhibitor!

    ⚠️ Use ARBs with CAUTION (small cross-reactivity risk ~10%)

    ⚠️ First-Dose Hypotensive Effect May Occur

    Some patients experience significant drop in BP after first dose

    Prevention:

    • Start with LOW dose
    • Take first dose at bedtime
    • Ensure patient is adequately hydrated
    • If patient on diuretic, may hold diuretic dose before starting ACE inhibitor

    Contraindications to ACE Inhibitors:

    • ⚠️ PREGNANCY: ABSOLUTELY CONTRAINDICATED!
      • Teratogenic - causes fetal harm
      • Especially dangerous in 2nd and 3rd trimester
      • Can cause:
        • Fetal renal dysfunction/failure
        • Oligohydramnios (decreased amniotic fluid)
        • Fetal/neonatal hypotension
        • Skull hypoplasia
        • Death
      • Women of childbearing age: Discuss contraception, pregnancy plans
      • If pregnancy detected: STOP immediately!
    • Bilateral Renal Artery Stenosis:
      • Kidneys depend on Angiotensin II for perfusion pressure
      • Blocking AII → severe ↓ in GFR → acute kidney failure
    • History of Angioedema:
      • From previous ACE inhibitor use
      • From any cause (increases risk)
    • Current Hyperkalemia (K+ >5.5 mEq/L)
    • Hypersensitivity/Allergy to ACE inhibitors

    🩺 Laboratory Monitoring for ACE Inhibitors:

    Regular monitoring is ESSENTIAL for safe use!

    ⚠️ ACE inhibitors can cause renal impairment, which can be identified with serum creatinine.

    ⚠️ ACE inhibitors can also cause hyperkalemia, so potassium levels need to be monitored.

    What to Monitor:

    • Serum Creatinine:
      • Should remain stable or ↓ slightly
      • Small increase (<30% from baseline) is acceptable
      • ⚠️ If ↑ >30% or continues rising: May need to ↓ dose or stop
    • BUN:
      • Monitor for increasing trends
      • Ratio of BUN:Cr also informative
    • Potassium (K+):
      • Goal: 3.5-5.0 mEq/L
      • Concern if: >5.5 mEq/L
      • Danger if: >6.0 mEq/L (cardiac arrhythmia risk)
    • Blood Pressure:
      • Regularly monitor to assess effectiveness
      • Watch for excessive drops
    • Urine protein (in diabetics or CKD patients):
      • Should ↓ with therapy
      • Spot urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio

    📅 Monitoring Schedule:

    Timepoint Labs to Check
    Baseline (before starting) Cr, BUN, K+, BP, (urine protein if DM/CKD)
    2-4 weeks after starting Cr, K+, BP
    After dose increases Cr, K+ in 1-2 weeks
    Stable therapy Cr, K+ every 6-12 months
    If concerns arise Check more frequently

    Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers (ARBs)

    🚫 MEMORY ANCHOR: "Block the signal at the receptor" - Prevent angiotensin II from binding

    ✅ How to Recognize ARBs:

    ALL ARBs end in "-SARTAN"

    Also called: Angiotensin II Blockers or AT₁ Receptor Blockers

    Well tolerated - often chosen when patients can't tolerate ACE inhibitors

    ⭐ DO NOT cause dry cough! (Unlike ACE inhibitors)

    📋 Complete List of ARBs:

    • 1. Losartan (Cozaar)
    • 2. Eprosartan (Teveten)
    • 3. Valsartan (Diovan)
    • 4. Irbesartan (Avapro)
    • 5. Candesartan (Atacand)
    • 6. Olmesartan (Benicar)
    • 7. Telmisartan (Micardis)
    • 8. Azilsartan (Edarbi)

    Mechanism of Action:

    1. ARBs affect primarily vascular smooth muscle and the adrenal gland
    2. Selectively block the binding of Angiotensin II to AT₁ receptors
      • AT₁ = Angiotensin II Type 1 receptors
      • These receptors mediate the harmful effects of AII
    3. Even though Angiotensin II is still produced (unlike with ACE inhibitors), it cannot exert its effects
      • Think: Lock and key - ARB blocks the lock so key (AII) can't fit
    4. Results:
      • Block vasoconstriction → vasodilation → ↓ SVR
      • Block secretion of aldosterone → ↓ Na+/H₂O retention → ↓ blood volume
      • ↓ Sympathetic activation
      • ↓ Cardiac and vascular remodeling
    5. NET EFFECT: ↓ SVR + ↓ Blood Volume → ↓ BP

    💡 Key Difference from ACE Inhibitors:

    • ACE Inhibitors: Prevent formation of Angiotensin II
    • ARBs: Let AII form, but prevent it from working
    • ARBs do NOT affect bradykinin
      • ACE inhibitors ↑ bradykinin → dry cough
      • ARBs don't affect bradykinin → NO cough!
      • This is why ARBs are preferred for patients who develop cough on ACE inhibitors

    💡 For hypertension alone, ACE inhibitors and ARBs are considered interchangeable - choose based on tolerance and cost!

    ⚠️ Important Notes:

    • DO NOT combine ACE inhibitor + ARB!
      • No additional benefit
      • Increased risk of hyperkalemia, hypotension, renal dysfunction
      • Studies showed increased adverse events
      • Previously done, now NOT recommended
    • ACE inhibitors generally have more long-term outcome data (been around longer)
    • For DM and HF, ACE inhibitors still considered first-line by most guidelines (more data)
    • ARBs are excellent alternative when ACE not tolerated

    Indications for ARBs:

    • Hypertension (first-line option, especially if ACE not tolerated)
    • Adjunctive drugs for treatment of Heart Failure
      • When ACE inhibitors not tolerated
      • Reduce hospitalization and mortality
    • Diabetic nephropathy (alternative to ACE inhibitors)
    • Post-MI (if ACE inhibitor not tolerated)
    • Chronic kidney disease with proteinuria
    • May be used alone or with other drugs such as diuretics, CCBs

    ⚠️ Adverse Effects of ARBs:

    Most Common Adverse Effects:

    • Chest Pain
    • Fatigue
    • Hypoglycemia (in diabetics)
    • Diarrhea
    • Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)
    • Anemia
    • Weakness
    • Dizziness
    • Headache

    ⭐ LESS Common than with ACE Inhibitors:

    • Hyperkalemia: Slightly less than ACE inhibitors
      • Still need to monitor K+ levels!
      • Same mechanism (↓ aldosterone)
    • Cough: MUCH less common - <2%
      • This is a MAJOR advantage!
      • When it occurs, usually milder than with ACE inhibitors
    • Angioedema: Lower risk (~0.1%)
      • Can still occur (rare)
      • ~10% cross-reactivity in patients with ACE inhibitor-induced angioedema

    ⚠️ Overdose Manifestations:

    • Hypotension (most common)
    • Tachycardia (reflex response to low BP)
    • Bradycardia (occurs less often)
    • Dizziness, lightheadedness

    Treatment:

    • Symptomatic and supportive care
    • IV fluids to expand blood volume
    • Positioning (Trendelenburg if needed)
    • Vasopressors if severe (rare)

    Contraindications & Cautions with ARBs:

    Contraindications:

    • ⚠️ Pregnancy: Teratogenic - same as ACE inhibitors
      • Can cause fetal renal dysfunction, oligohydramnios, death
      • STOP immediately if pregnancy detected
    • Known hypersensitivity to ARBs
    • Bilateral renal artery stenosis

    Use with CAUTION in:

    • Renal dysfunction/impairment:
      • May worsen kidney function
      • Monitor Cr, BUN, GFR closely
      • May need dose adjustment
    • Hepatic dysfunction:
      • Most ARBs are metabolized by liver
      • May need dose reduction
    • Renal artery stenosis (unilateral or bilateral)
    • Volume depletion/Dehydration:
      • Risk of excessive hypotension
      • Correct volume status before starting
    • Hyperkalemia or risk factors for hyperkalemia

    ⚠️ NOT safe for breastfeeding women

    ⚠️ Should NOT be used in pregnancy

    Losartan (Cozaar) - Prototype ARB

    📌 Losartan (Cozaar)

    First ARB approved in the U.S. (1995)

    Most studied ARB

    Benefits:

    • Beneficial in patients with hypertension and heart failure
    • Unique benefit: Also lowers uric acid levels
      • May be preferred in patients with gout or hyperuricemia
      • Only ARB with this property
    • Once-daily dosing (usually)
    • Can take with or without food
    • Generic available (cost-effective)
    • Well-tolerated

    Use with Caution:

    • Patients with renal dysfunction/impairment
    • Patients with hepatic dysfunction
      • Losartan is a prodrug - needs liver activation
      • May need dose reduction in liver disease
    • Patients with renal artery stenosis

    Avoid in:

    • NOT safe for breastfeeding women
    • Should NOT be used in pregnancy - teratogenic

    Typical Dosing:

    • Hypertension: Start 50 mg once daily, may increase to 100 mg
    • Heart Failure: Start 25 mg once daily, titrate up
    • Diabetic Nephropathy: Start 50 mg once daily, increase to 100 mg
    • Available in combination with HCTZ (Hyzaar)

    🔬 Calcium Channel Blockers & Diuretics

    Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)

    😌 MEMORY ANCHOR: "Relax the muscle wall" - Block calcium entry to prevent contraction

    Mechanism of Action:

    1. Cause smooth muscle relaxation by blocking calcium channels
    2. Prevent calcium entry into smooth muscle cells
    3. Calcium is REQUIRED for muscle contraction
      • Calcium binds to troponin → allows actin-myosin interaction → contraction
      • No calcium = no contraction
    4. Results in:
      • Decreased peripheral smooth muscle tone → vasodilation
      • Decreased SVR
      • Decreased BP
      • Some CCBs also decrease heart rate and contractility

    Two Main Classes of CCBs:

    Class Drugs Primary Effects
    Dihydropyridines (DHPs) • Amlodipine (Norvasc) ⭐
    • Nifedipine
    • Felodipine
    • Nicardipine    		 Mainly affect BLOOD VESSELS
    • Powerful vasodilation
    • Minimal effect on heart
    • Do NOT slow heart rate
    • Can cause reflex tachycardia
    Non-Dihydropyridines • Diltiazem (Cardizem)
    • Verapamil (Calan)    		 Affect BOTH vessels AND heart
    • Moderate vasodilation
    • ↓ Heart rate (like beta blocker)
    • ↓ Contractility
    • Slow AV conduction

    ⚠️ Important Drug Interaction:

    DO NOT combine Non-DHP CCBs with Beta Blockers!

    • Both slow heart rate
    • Both decrease contractility
    • Both slow AV conduction
    • Risk of:
      • Severe bradycardia
      • Heart block
      • Heart failure exacerbation

    💡 DHP CCBs (like amlodipine) CAN be safely combined with beta blockers!

    Primary Uses of CCBs:

    • Hypertension - First-line option
      • Amlodipine (Norvasc): Most commonly prescribed CCB for HTN
      • Particularly effective in African American patients
      • Particularly effective in elderly patients
    • Angina (Chest Pain):
      • Angina = chest pain from reduced blood flow to heart muscle
      • CCBs reduce cardiac oxygen demand
      • CCBs increase coronary blood flow (dilate coronary arteries)
    • Dysrhythmias:
      • Diltiazem and verapamil (non-DHPs)
      • Control rate in atrial fibrillation/flutter
      • Treat supraventricular tachycardia (SVT)
    • Raynaud's Disease:
      • Vasospastic disorder affecting fingers/toes
      • CCBs prevent vasospasm
      • Improve circulation and reduce attacks
    • Migraine Prevention:
      • Verapamil most commonly used
      • Reduces frequency and severity of migraines
    • Prevention of cerebral artery spasms:
      • After subarachnoid hemorrhage
      • Nimodipine: Specific for this indication

    ⭐ Amlodipine (Norvasc) - Most Commonly Used CCB:

    • DHP class (mainly vasodilation)
    • Excellent for hypertension
    • Once-daily dosing (long half-life ~30-50 hours)
    • Consistent BP control over 24 hours
    • Well-tolerated
    • Can take with or without food
    • Generic available (inexpensive)
    • Can be combined with ACE/ARB, beta blockers, diuretics
    • Available in combination pills (e.g., amlodipine/benazepril)

    ⚠️ Adverse Effects of CCBs:

    Common to ALL CCBs:

    • Headache: Especially when starting (vasodilation effect)
      • Usually improves after 1-2 weeks
    • Dizziness/Lightheadedness
    • Flushing: Feeling of warmth, facial redness
    • Hypotension: Especially orthostatic

    ⭐ Specific to DHP CCBs (Amlodipine, Nifedipine):

    • Peripheral Edema (ankle/leg swelling) - VERY COMMON
      • Occurs in 10-30% of patients
      • Due to vasodilation of precapillary vessels more than postcapillary
      • Fluid leaks into tissues
      • NOT from heart failure!
      • Does NOT respond to diuretics
      • Management:
        • Elevate legs
        • Compression stockings
        • Reduce dose
        • Add ACE inhibitor or ARB (may help)
        • Switch to different CCB or drug class if severe
    • Reflex Tachycardia:
      • Vasodilation → ↓ BP → baroreceptor response → ↑ HR
      • More common with short-acting nifedipine
      • Less with amlodipine (long-acting)
    • Gingival Hyperplasia: Gum overgrowth (rare)
      • Good dental hygiene important

    Specific to Non-DHP CCBs (Diltiazem, Verapamil):

    • Bradycardia: Slow heart rate
    • AV Block: Slowed conduction through AV node
    • Constipation: VERY common with verapamil (30-40% of patients!)
      • Can be severe
      • Increase fiber, fluids, stool softeners
    • Worsening of heart failure: Negative inotropic effect
    • Less peripheral edema than DHPs

    Contraindications:

    All CCBs:

    • Known hypersensitivity
    • Severe hypotension

    Non-DHP CCBs (Diltiazem, Verapamil) ALSO contraindicated in:

    • Severe bradycardia (HR <50 bpm)
    • Heart block (2nd or 3rd degree without pacemaker)
    • Acute heart failure or severe HF (negative inotrope)
    • Sick sinus syndrome
    • Concurrent use with beta blockers (relative contraindication - high risk)
    • Atrial fibrillation with WPW syndrome (can precipitate V-fib)

    Use with Caution:

    • Hepatic impairment (metabolized by liver - may need dose reduction)
    • Elderly patients (more sensitive to effects)
    • Concurrent use of other negative chronotropes or inotropes

    Diuretics for Hypertension

    💧 MEMORY ANCHOR: "Drain the tank" - Diuretics decrease fluid volume

    ⭐ JNC-8 Recommendation:

    Thiazide diuretics are FIRST-LINE antihypertensives

    Exception: NOT first-line in patients with kidney disease (GFR <30) - use loop diuretics or ACE/ARB instead

    Mechanism of Action:

    1. Decrease plasma and extracellular fluid volumes
      • Increase urine output (diuresis)
      • Excrete sodium and water
    2. Results:
      • Decreased Preload: Less volume returning to heart
      • Decreased Cardiac Output: Less blood volume to pump
      • Decreased Total Peripheral Resistance: Long-term vascular effects
    3. Overall Effect:
      • Decreased workload of the heart
      • Decreased Blood Pressure

    Hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ) - Most Common Thiazide:

    • Inexpensive, effective, well-tolerated
    • Once-daily dosing (usually)
    • Typical dose: 12.5-25 mg daily
      • Higher doses (>25 mg) don't significantly ↑ BP reduction
      • But DO increase side effects
    • Often combined with ACE/ARB or beta blocker
    • Many combination pills available
    • Take in morning to avoid nighttime urination

    ⚠️ Adverse Effects of Diuretics:

    Thiazide Diuretics:

    • Hypokalemia (low potassium) - MOST IMPORTANT!
      • Weakness, fatigue
      • Muscle cramps
      • Cardiac arrhythmias
      • Monitor K+ levels
      • Teach: Eat K+-rich foods (bananas, oranges, potatoes, spinach)
      • May need K+ supplement
    • Hyponatremia (low sodium)
      • Confusion, lethargy
      • More common in elderly
    • Hypomagnesemia (low magnesium)
    • Hyperuricemia → Gout attacks
      • Avoid in patients with history of gout
    • Hyperglycemia: Can worsen diabetes
      • Monitor blood glucose in diabetics
    • Hyperlipidemia: ↑ Cholesterol, ↑ triglycerides (mild)
    • Hypercalcemia: ↑ Calcium (usually mild)
    • Sexual dysfunction in men (erectile dysfunction)
    • Photosensitivity: Increased sun sensitivity
      • Teach: Use sunscreen, protective clothing
    • Dizziness, orthostatic hypotension
    • Frequent urination (especially initially)

    Loop Diuretics:

    • Similar to thiazides but more pronounced:
      • Hypokalemia
      • Hyponatremia
      • Hypomagnesemia
      • Hypocalcemia (opposite of thiazides!)
      • Hyperuricemia
    • Ototoxicity: Hearing loss (rare, with high IV doses)
    • More profound volume depletion → hypotension

    Potassium-Sparing Diuretics:

    • Hyperkalemia - OPPOSITE problem!
      • Monitor K+ closely
      • Avoid K+ supplements and salt substitutes
      • Caution with ACE/ARBs (also ↑ K+)
    • Spironolactone specific:
      • Gynecomastia (breast development in men)
      • Menstrual irregularities
      • Sexual dysfunction
      • (Anti-androgen effects)

    Contraindications:

    Thiazide Diuretics:

    • Severe renal impairment (GFR <30 mL/min)
      • Not effective when kidneys severely impaired
      • Use loop diuretic instead
    • Anuria (no urine output)
    • Hypersensitivity to sulfa drugs (thiazides are sulfonamides)

    Potassium-Sparing Diuretics:

    • Hyperkalemia (K+ >5.0 mEq/L)
    • Severe renal impairment
    • Concurrent use of other K+-sparing agents or ACE/ARBs (relative contraindication)
    • Addison's disease (already have high K+)

    💊 Clinical Pearls - Diuretic Use:

    • Take in MORNING to avoid nocturia (nighttime urination)
      • Peak effect: 2-4 hours after dose
      • If taken at bedtime → up all night urinating!
    • Monitor electrolytes regularly
      • Baseline, then 2-4 weeks, then periodically
      • Especially: K+, Na+, Mg²+
    • Ensure adequate hydration
      • But not excessive (defeats purpose)
    • Dietary potassium important with thiazides/loops
      • Bananas, oranges, tomatoes, potatoes, spinach, beans
    • Combination with ACE/ARB very common
      • Synergistic BP-lowering
      • ACE/ARB offsets K+ loss from diuretic
      • Many combination pills available

    🌡️ Vasodilators & Other Antihypertensive Drugs

    Vasodilators

    🥤 MEMORY ANCHOR: "Make the straw bigger" - Dilate vessels to reduce resistance

    Mechanism of Action:

    1. Directly relax arteriolar and/or venous smooth muscle
      • Work on blood vessel walls directly
      • Not via receptors or enzymes
    2. Results in:
      • Decreased SVR (arteriolar dilation)
      • Decreased Afterload
      • Peripheral Vasodilation
      • Venous dilation → ↓ preload (some vasodilators)
    3. "Makes the Tank Bigger" → Lowers the Pressure

    💡 Think of it this way:

    Same amount of blood in a BIGGER container = LOWER pressure

    Vasodilators expand the blood vessels (make the container bigger) so pressure drops

    🚨 CRITICAL: Vasodilators are ALWAYS Used in Combination!

    ⚠️ NEVER used as monotherapy (alone) for chronic HTN

    Why? Two major problems when used alone:

    1. Reflex Tachycardia:
      • Vasodilation → ↓ BP → baroreceptors sense low BP
      • Body responds: ↑ sympathetic activation
      • Heart rate increases dramatically (compensatory mechanism)
      • ↑ Cardiac workload (defeats purpose!)
      • Solution: Add BETA BLOCKER to prevent tachycardia
    2. Sodium and Water Retention:
      • ↓ BP → kidneys sense low perfusion
      • Activate RAAS
      • Retain sodium and water (compensatory mechanism)
      • Blood volume increases → BP rises again
      • Solution: Add DIURETIC to prevent fluid retention

    ✅ Typical Combination: Vasodilator + Beta Blocker + Diuretic

    This "triple therapy" is common for resistant hypertension

    📋 Common Vasodilators:

    • Hydralazine (Apresoline)
    • Minoxidil (Rogaine)
    • Diazoxide (Hyperstat)
    • Sodium Nitroprusside (Nitropress)

    Indications:

    • Treatment of hypertension
      • Usually third-line or later
      • When other agents have failed
      • Must be used in combination with other drugs
    • ⭐ Hypertensive Emergencies:
      • Sodium nitroprusside IV: Drug of choice
      • Diazoxide IV: Alternative
      • Reserved for ICU/emergency management
      • Need continuous BP monitoring

    ⚠️ Adverse Effects of Vasodilators:

    Common to All:

    • Dizziness
    • Headache: Due to vasodilation (very common)
      • Headache = throbbing, pounding headache from dilated cerebral vessels
    • Dyspnea: Shortness of breath
      • Dyspnea = difficulty breathing, feeling short of breath
    • Reflex Tachycardia
    • Fluid Retention/Edema
    • Hypotension (especially orthostatic)

    Hydralazine Specific:

    • Dizziness, headache
    • Anxiety, palpitations
    • Tachycardia
    • Edema
    • Dyspnea
    • Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea
    • Drug-Induced Lupus Syndrome: (rare, with high doses/long-term use)
      • Joint pain, fever, rash
      • More common in slow acetylators
      • Reversible when drug stopped
    • Vitamin B6 deficiency: Peripheral neuropathy
    • Hepatitis (rare)
    • Rash

    Minoxidil Specific:

    • Hypertrichosis: Excessive hair growth
      • Occurs in most patients (80%)
      • Affects face, arms, back
      • Reversible when stopped
      • This is why it's sold as Rogaine for hair regrowth!
    • T-wave changes on ECG
    • Pericardial effusion: Fluid around heart (serious!)
      • Can progress to tamponade
      • Monitor closely
    • Angina
    • Breast tenderness
    • Rash
    • Thrombocytopenia (low platelets)

    Sodium Nitroprusside Specific:

    • Bradycardia
    • Decreased platelet aggregation
    • Rash
    • Hypothyroidism (with prolonged use)
    • Hypotension (can be severe if infusion too fast)
    • Methemoglobinemia (rare)
    • ⚠️ Cyanide Toxicity: Rare but potentially fatal
      • Nitroprusside metabolizes to cyanide
      • Risk with high doses or prolonged infusion (>72 hours)
      • Symptoms: metabolic acidosis, confusion, seizures
      • Monitor thiocyanate levels with prolonged use

    Specific Vasodilators

    📌 Hydralazine (Apresoline)

    Two Routes - Different Uses:

    1. ORAL Hydralazine:

    • Use: Routine cases of essential hypertension
    • Chronic management
    • Part of triple therapy (hydralazine + beta blocker + diuretic)
    • Typical dose: 10-50 mg 2-4 times daily
    • Take with meals to enhance absorption

    2. INJECTABLE Hydralazine (IV/IM):

    • Use: Hypertensive emergencies
    • Rapid BP reduction needed
    • Hospital/ICU setting
    • Onset: 10-30 minutes
    • Often used in pregnancy (eclampsia/severe preeclampsia)

    🤔 Why use ORAL vs INJECTABLE?

    • Oral: Chronic HTN management, outpatient, stable BP control
    • Injectable: Emergencies, rapid BP reduction, inpatient

    ⭐ BiDil (Hydralazine + Isosorbide Dinitrate):

    • Combination product
    • Specifically indicated as adjunct for treatment of HF in African-American patients
    • Improves survival in this population
    • Used with standard HF therapy (ACE/ARB, beta blocker, diuretic)
    📌 Minoxidil (Rogaine)

    Use for HTN:

    • Severe, refractory hypertension
      • When all other medications have failed
      • Last-line agent
    • Very potent vasodilator
    • ALWAYS combined with beta blocker + diuretic
    • Oral only (for HTN)

    Other Use:

    • Topical for hair regrowth!
      • Sold OTC as Rogaine
      • Stimulates hair follicles
      • Different concentration than for BP

    Side Effect becomes Treatment:

    The hypertrichosis (hair growth) side effect from oral minoxidil led to its development as a topical hair regrowth product!

    📌 Sodium Nitroprusside (Nitropress)

    ⚠️ HIGH-ALERT MEDICATION

    ICU Use Only

    Route: IV INFUSION ONLY

    Use:

    • Severe hypertensive emergencies
    • Rapid, precise BP reduction needed
    • Intensive care setting
    • Titrated to effect by IV infusion
      • Continuous BP monitoring required
      • Arterial line usually placed
      • Adjust drip rate to achieve target BP

    Special Handling:

    • Light-sensitive: Must cover IV bag and tubing with opaque material (foil, brown bag)
    • Prepare fresh every 24 hours
    • Use within 24 hours of preparation
    • Solution should be faint brownish tint - discard if dark

    Contraindications:

    • Known hypersensitivity
    • Severe heart failure
    • Inadequate cerebral perfusion
      • Especially during neurosurgical procedures
      • Need adequate cerebral blood flow
    • Tobacco amblyopia (vision problem)
    • Congenital optic atrophy

    Advantages:

    • Onset: IMMEDIATE (seconds)
    • Offset: RAPID (1-2 minutes when stopped)
    • Precise titration possible
    • Very effective

    Disadvantages:

    • Requires ICU monitoring
    • Risk of excessive hypotension
    • Cyanide toxicity risk (prolonged use)
    • Expensive
    • Complex handling requirements

    Other Antihypertensive Drugs

    ⏹️ MEMORY ANCHOR: "Stop the cascade at the start" - Direct renin inhibitors block RAAS at the beginning
    📌 Eplerenone (Inspra)

    Class: Selective Aldosterone Blocker (newer class)

    Mechanism:

    • Reduces BP by blocking the actions of aldosterone at its receptors
    • Acts on aldosterone receptors in:
      • Kidney
      • Heart
      • Blood vessels
      • Brain
    • Prevents sodium/water retention
    • Reduces vascular remodeling

    Indications:

    • Routine treatment of hypertension
    • Post-MI heart failure
      • Reduces mortality
      • Prevents further cardiac remodeling

    Advantage over Spironolactone:

    • More selective for aldosterone receptor
    • Fewer anti-androgen effects
      • Less gynecomastia
      • Less sexual dysfunction
      • Better tolerated, especially in men

    ⚠️ Contraindications:

    • Hyperkalemia (K+ >5.6 mEq/L)
      • Will worsen hyperkalemia
      • Can cause life-threatening arrhythmias
    • Severe renal impairment (CrCl <30 mL/min)
    • Use of K+-sparing diuretics
    • Use of strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (ketoconazole, itraconazole, etc.)

    Caution with:

    • ACE inhibitors or ARBs (also ↑ K+)
    • NSAIDs (affect kidney function)
    • Diabetes

    ⚠️ Monitor potassium closely!

    📌 Treatment of Pulmonary Hypertension

    Pulmonary hypertension = High blood pressure in the arteries of the lungs

    Different from systemic hypertension - requires specialized treatment

    Bosentan (Tracleer):

    • Indication: Pulmonary artery hypertension in patients with moderate to severe HF
    • Mechanism: Blocks receptors of the hormone endothelin
      • Endothelin is a potent vasoconstrictor
      • Blocking it → vasodilation of pulmonary arteries
    • ⚠️ Black Box Warnings:
      • Hepatotoxicity: Liver damage - monitor LFTs monthly
      • Teratogenicity: Causes birth defects - contraindicated in pregnancy

    Other Drugs for Pulmonary Hypertension:

    • Prostacyclin analogs:
      • Epoprostenol (IV continuous infusion)
      • Treprostinil (Remodulin) - IV, subcutaneous, or inhaled
      • Iloprost (inhaled)
      • Selexipag (oral)
    • Endothelin receptor antagonists:
      • Bosentan
      • Ambrisentan
      • Macitentan
    • PDE-5 Inhibitors: Sildenafil, Tadalafil (discussed below)

    💡 Understanding Combination Therapy

    Why Use Combinations Instead of a Single Medication?

    Advantages of Combination Therapy:

    1. Attack BP from Multiple Angles:
      • Different mechanisms = synergistic effect
      • Example: ACE inhibitor (↓ RAAS) + CCB (vasodilation) + Diuretic (↓ volume)
    2. Use Lower Doses of Each Drug:
      • Lower doses = fewer side effects
      • Better tolerated
      • Better adherence
    3. Counteract Compensatory Mechanisms:
      • Vasodilator causes tachycardia → Add beta blocker
      • Vasodilator causes fluid retention → Add diuretic
      • Diuretic causes K+ loss → Add ACE inhibitor (retains K+)
    4. More Patients Achieve Goal BP:
      • Many patients need 2-3 drugs for control
      • This is NORMAL and expected!
    5. Address Multiple Comorbidities:
      • Example: HTN + DM + HF → ACE inhibitor + Beta blocker + Diuretic
      • Each drug treats multiple conditions

    Common Effective Combinations:

    • ACE inhibitor + Thiazide diuretic (very common)
      • Synergistic BP reduction
      • ACE offsets K+ loss from diuretic
      • Many combo pills: Lisinopril/HCTZ, Enalapril/HCTZ
    • ARB + CCB
      • Complementary mechanisms
      • CCB may reduce ARB-induced edema
      • Combo pills: Valsartan/amlodipine, Olmesartan/amlodipine
    • Beta blocker + Diuretic
      • Especially post-MI
      • Synergistic effects
    • ACE/ARB + CCB + Diuretic (Triple therapy)
      • For resistant HTN
      • Attack from all angles
    • Vasodilator + Beta blocker + Diuretic
      • For severe HTN
      • Counteracts vasodilator side effects

    ⚡ Know WHY the combination is being used vs. a singular medication!

    Each drug in a combination serves a specific purpose - understand the rationale!

    • Family history of HTN, CVD, stroke, DM
    • Lifestyle factors: diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol, stress
    • Pregnancy status (women of childbearing age)

    2. Complete Head-to-Toe Physical Examination:

    • Vital Signs:
      • BP (both arms, sitting and standing for baseline orthostatic)
      • Heart rate and rhythm
      • Respiratory rate
      • Temperature
      • O₂ saturation
    • Cardiovascular:
      • Heart sounds (murmurs, S3/S4)
      • Peripheral pulses
      • Capillary refill
      • Edema (location, severity)
      • JVD (jugular venous distention)
    • Respiratory:
      • Lung sounds (crackles = fluid overload)
      • Respiratory effort
    • Neurological:
      • LOC, orientation
      • Cranial nerves
      • Motor/sensory function
    • Other:
      • Weight (baseline for monitoring)
      • Signs of end-organ damage

    3. Review Laboratory Values:

    • Baseline labs BEFORE starting antihypertensives:
      • Renal function: BUN, Creatinine, eGFR
      • Electrolytes: Na+, K+, Cl⁻, CO₂, Mg²+, Ca²+
      • Glucose: Fasting blood glucose or HbA1c
      • Lipid panel: Total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides
      • Liver function: AST, ALT, bilirubin (especially if using methyldopa)
      • CBC: Baseline hemoglobin, WBC, platelets
      • Urine: Urinalysis, microalbumin (if DM or CKD)
      • Other: Uric acid (if using diuretics), TSH (if symptoms)

    4. Assess for Contraindications:

    • Review contraindications specific to each drug class
    • Pregnancy status (ACE/ARBs contraindicated)
    • Asthma/COPD (avoid beta blockers)
    • Hyperkalemia (avoid ACE/ARBs, K+-sparing diuretics)
    • Severe renal impairment (caution with many drugs)

    5. Assess for Conditions Requiring Cautious Use:

    • Renal or hepatic dysfunction
    • Electrolyte imbalances
    • Gout (caution with thiazides)
    • DM (some drugs affect glucose)
    • Depression (caution with centrally-acting drugs, beta blockers)

    💊 Medication Administration

    🚨 CRITICAL Nursing Actions:

    1. ⚠️ DON'T MISS A DOSE!

    • Educate patients: Taking medications EXACTLY as prescribed is crucial
    • Missing doses → BP spikes → increased risk of stroke, MI, HF
    • Set up pill organizers, reminders, alarms
    • Address barriers to adherence (cost, side effects, complexity)

    2. ⚠️ NEVER DOUBLE UP!

    • If a dose is missed:
      • Check with physician for specific instructions
      • General rule: Take as soon as remembered UNLESS close to next dose
      • NEVER take two doses together!
      • Risk of severe hypotension

    3. ⚠️ DO NOT STOP ABRUPTLY!

    • ⚠️ NEVER stop antihypertensives suddenly without medical supervision!
    • Risk of rebound hypertension: Severe, rapid BP increase
      • Especially: Clonidine, methyldopa, beta blockers
      • Can cause: Hypertensive crisis, stroke, MI, death
    • If discontinuing: MUST taper gradually over days to weeks
    • Educate patients: Don't stop because "feeling better" or side effects
    • Discuss concerns with provider - may switch to different drug

    Timing and Administration Tips:

    Most Oral Antihypertensives:

    • Give with meals
      • Absorption is more gradual → more effective BP control
      • Reduces GI upset
      • Exceptions: Captopril (1 hour before meals), Moexipril (1 hour before)
    • Can take with or without food: Most ACE/ARBs, CCBs, beta blockers

    Diuretics:

    • Take in the MORNING (before noon)
      • Avoid nighttime dosing → prevents nocturia (waking up to urinate)
      • If BID dosing: Morning and early afternoon (not evening)
      • Peak effect 2-4 hours → plan bathroom access

    Transdermal Patches (Clonidine):

    • Apply to hairless area of upper arm or chest
    • Change weekly (same day each week)
    • Rotate sites
    • Can bathe/shower with patch on
    • If patch falls off: Apply new patch, monitor BP closely

    IV Antihypertensives:

    • Administer with EXTREME CAUTION
    • Use IV pump for precise control
    • Continuous BP monitoring (often arterial line)
    • ICU setting usually required
    • Have emergency equipment available

    ⚠️ Before Giving Each Dose - Check:

    Blood Pressure:

    • Measure BP before administration
    • Hold parameters: Know facility policy
      • Typically: Hold if SBP <100 or <90 mmHg
      • Notify provider
      • Document
    • Assess for symptomatic hypotension (dizziness, lightheadedness)

    Heart Rate (for beta blockers, non-DHP CCBs):

    • Measure apical pulse for 1 full minute
    • Hold parameters: Typically hold if HR <50-60 bpm
      • Check facility policy
      • Notify provider
    • Assess rhythm (irregular = concerning)

    Signs of Dehydration (for diuretics):

    • Assess hydration status
    • Monitor I&Os (intake and output)
    • Daily weights (same time, same scale, same clothing)
    • Assess for: dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, concentrated urine

    📚 Patient Education - CRITICAL Information

    ✅ Lifestyle Modifications (Non-Pharmacological Therapy):

    ⭐ Medication is only PART of therapy!

    1. Diet - DASH Diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension):

    • ↑ Increase:
      • Fruits and vegetables (8-10 servings/day)
      • Whole grains
      • Low-fat dairy
      • Lean proteins (fish, poultry, beans)
      • Nuts and legumes
      • Foods rich in K+, Mg²+, Ca²+
    • ↓ Decrease/Avoid:
      • SODIUM: <2,300 mg/day (ideally <1,500 mg/day)
        • Read food labels!
        • Avoid processed foods, canned soups, deli meats, fast food
        • Don't add salt to food
        • Use herbs/spices instead
      • Saturated fats and trans fats
      • Red meat
      • Sweets and sugary drinks
      • Alcohol (if excessive)
        • Moderate: ≤2 drinks/day (men), ≤1 drink/day (women)

    2. Weight Management:

    • Goal: BMI 18.5-24.9 kg/m²
    • Waist circumference: <40" (men), <35" (women)
    • Even 5-10 lb weight loss can significantly lower BP
    • Every 10 kg (22 lb) lost → ↓ SBP by 5-20 mmHg

    3. Regular Physical Activity:

    • Goal: 150 minutes/week of moderate aerobic exercise
      • Or 75 minutes/week of vigorous exercise
      • Plus strength training 2 days/week
    • Examples: Brisk walking, swimming, cycling, dancing
    • ⭐ Supervised exercise program recommended
      • Especially if starting new exercise routine
      • Cardiac rehabilitation if post-MI or high-risk
    • Build up gradually
    • Find activities you enjoy (better adherence)

    4. Smoking Cessation:

    • QUIT smoking completely
      • No amount is safe
      • Smoking raises BP acutely and damages vessels
    • Resources: Nicotine replacement, medications (bupropion, varenicline), counseling, support groups
    • Benefits begin immediately upon quitting

    5. Alcohol Moderation:

    • Limit to: ≤2 drinks/day (men), ≤1 drink/day (women)
    • 1 drink = 12 oz beer, 5 oz wine, 1.5 oz spirits
    • Excessive alcohol raises BP

    6. Stress Management:

    • Identify stressors
    • Techniques: Deep breathing, meditation, yoga, progressive muscle relaxation
    • Adequate sleep (7-9 hours/night)
    • Counseling if needed
    • Time management
    • Social support

    7. Adequate Sleep:

    • 7-9 hours per night
    • Screen for sleep apnea if symptoms (snoring, daytime sleepiness)
    • Good sleep hygiene

    ⚠️ Orthostatic Hypotension Prevention:

    ⚠️ SAFETY: "Low and Slow" - Rise slowly from lying → sitting → standing

    Teach patients to CHANGE POSITIONS SLOWLY:

    Why? Many antihypertensives cause orthostatic hypotension (sudden BP drop when standing)

    How to prevent falls and injury:

    1. From lying to sitting:
      • Sit on edge of bed for 1-2 minutes
      • Dangle legs over side
      • Wait for dizziness to pass
    2. From sitting to standing:
      • Stand up SLOWLY
      • Hold onto furniture/wall for support
      • Stand still for 30-60 seconds before walking
    3. If dizziness occurs:
      • Sit or lie down immediately
      • Don't try to "push through"
      • Wait until symptoms subside

    Additional tips:

    • Avoid prolonged standing
    • Sit down when showering if dizzy
    • Use handrails on stairs
    • Adequate hydration
    • Compression stockings may help
    • Avoid alcohol (worsens orthostatic hypotension)

    🚨 When to Seek Medical Attention:

    Instruct patients to REPORT the following symptoms immediately:

    • Cardiovascular:
      • Chest pain or pressure
      • Palpitations (feeling heart racing or skipping beats)
      • Severe dizziness or fainting
    • Respiratory:
      • Unusual shortness of breath
      • Difficulty breathing (dyspnea)
      • Wheezing (especially if on beta blocker with asthma/COPD)
    • Edema:
      • Swelling of feet, ankles (new or worsening)
      • Swelling of face or around eyes
      • Rapid weight gain (>2-3 lbs in a day or 5 lbs in a week)
    • Weight Changes:
      • Rapid weight gain (fluid retention)
      • Unexplained weight loss
    • Extreme Fatigue:
      • Excessive fatigue interfering with daily activities
      • New or worsening weakness
    • ⚠️ ANGIOEDEMA (ACE Inhibitors/ARBs):
      • Swelling of lips, tongue, throat, face
      • Difficulty swallowing
      • Difficulty breathing
      • → STOP medication immediately, call 911!
    • Persistent dry cough (ACE inhibitors)
      • May need to switch to ARB
    • Signs of hyperkalemia:
      • Muscle weakness
      • Palpitations
      • Tingling/numbness
    • Signs of hypokalemia (diuretics):
      • Muscle cramps, weakness
      • Irregular heartbeat

    💬 Address Sexual Dysfunction Concerns:

    ⚠️ IMPORTANT: Male patients may not be aware that impotence is an expected effect

    This significantly influences compliance with drug therapy!

    Nursing Actions:

    • Bring it up proactively - don't wait for patient to mention it
      • Many patients are embarrassed to discuss
      • May stop medications without telling you
    • Normalize the conversation:
      • "Some blood pressure medications can affect sexual function. Has this been an issue for you?"
      • Use matter-of-fact tone
      • Private, confidential discussion
    • Educate:
      • Common with certain medications (beta blockers, diuretics, centrally-acting drugs)
      • NOT a personal failing
      • Treatable!
    • Solutions:
      • May be able to switch to different medication class
        • ACE/ARBs usually don't cause sexual dysfunction
        • Nebivolol (beta blocker) has less sexual dysfunction
      • Adjust dose
      • PDE-5 inhibitors (Viagra, Cialis) may help (if no contraindications)
      • Encourage discussion with provider
    • ⚠️ DON'T let patient stop medication without discussing with provider!

    💡 Affects BOTH men and women - decreased libido, arousal issues in women too!

    🛁 Hot Tubs, Hot Showers, Hot Weather - Say NO!

    ⚠️ Teach patients that the following can aggravate low BP, leading to fainting and injury:

    • ❌ Hot tubs
      • Heat → vasodilation → ↓ BP
      • Combined with antihypertensives → excessive ↓ BP
      • Risk of drowning if fainting in water!
      • AVOID hot tubs entirely
    • ⚠️ Hot showers or baths
      • Take warm (not hot) showers
      • Keep bathroom door unlocked
      • Sit down if dizzy
      • Use shower chair if needed
    • ☀️ Hot weather
      • Stay hydrated
      • Stay in air conditioning when possible
      • Avoid prolonged sun exposure
      • Be extra cautious
    • 🧍 Prolonged sitting or standing
      • Blood pools in legs → ↓ venous return → ↓ BP
      • Take breaks
      • Flex calf muscles while standing
      • Change positions regularly
    • 🏃 Physical exercise
      • Can cause hypotension, especially initially
      • Start slowly, build up gradually
      • Stay hydrated
      • Cool down properly
      • If dizzy → sit/lie down until symptoms subside
    • 🍺 Alcohol ingestion
      • Alcohol → vasodilation → ↓ BP
      • Combined with antihypertensives → excessive ↓ BP
      • Also impairs judgment (may not recognize symptoms)
      • Limit alcohol as recommended

    If symptoms occur (dizzy, lightheaded, weak):

    • Sit or lie down IMMEDIATELY until symptoms subside
    • Don't try to "push through"
    • Move to cool environment if hot
    • Sip water
    • Don't stand up until feeling completely better

    📝 Other Important Patient Teaching:

    Medication Management:

    • Keep a medication list with names, doses, times
    • Bring to all medical appointments
    • Don't take ANY other medications without approval:
      • Including over-the-counter drugs
      • Herbals and supplements
      • Get physician's approval FIRST
      • Many interactions possible
    • Refill medications BEFORE running out
      • Set reminder 1 week before
      • Use mail-order or auto-refill if helpful
    • If experiencing serious adverse effects:
      • Contact physician immediately
      • Don't just stop the medication
      • May need dose adjustment or different drug

    Home Blood Pressure Monitoring:

    • Encourage regular BP checks at home
    • Teach proper technique:
      • Use validated, calibrated home BP monitor
      • Rest 5 minutes before measuring
      • Sit with back supported, feet flat on floor
      • Arm supported at heart level
      • No talking during measurement
      • Take 2-3 readings 1 minute apart, average them
      • Same time each day (consistency)
    • Keep a journal/log of BP readings
      • Date, time, BP reading
      • Note any symptoms
      • Bring to appointments
    • Target BP: Know your goal (discuss with provider)

    Follow-Up Care:

    • Keep all scheduled appointments
    • Regular lab work as ordered
    • Report any new or concerning symptoms
    • Annual eye exams (check for retinopathy)
    • Regular dental care (some drugs cause gum issues)

    Emergency Preparedness:

    • Know signs of hypertensive emergency:
      • BP >180/120 with symptoms
      • Severe headache, confusion, vision changes
      • Chest pain, shortness of breath
      • Seizures, severe anxiety
      • → Call 911 immediately!
    • Wear medical alert bracelet (optional but helpful)
    • Keep medication list in wallet/purse

    🩺 Ongoing Monitoring & Evaluation

    Monitor for Adverse Effects:

    • Dizziness, orthostatic hypotension
    • Fatigue, weakness
    • Electrolyte imbalances (especially K+)
    • Sexual dysfunction
    • Depression, mood changes
    • Dry cough (ACE inhibitors)
    • Peripheral edema (CCBs)
    • Bradycardia (beta blockers, non-DHP CCBs)
    • Any drug-specific adverse effects

    Monitor for Toxic Effects:

    • Severe hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg with symptoms)
    • Severe bradycardia (HR <50 bpm or symptomatic)
    • Angioedema (ACE/ARBs)
    • Hyperkalemia or hypokalemia
    • Renal dysfunction (↑ Cr, ↓ GFR)
    • Liver toxicity (↑ LFTs)

    Monitor for Therapeutic Effects:

    • Primary goal: BP at target
      • General: <140/90 mmHg
      • DM or CKD: <140/90 mmHg
      • Elderly (>60): <150/90 mmHg
      • Individualized based on patient
    • Reduction in symptoms:
      • Headaches
      • Dizziness
      • Epistaxis (nosebleeds)
    • Prevention of complications:
      • No stroke, MI, HF
      • Stable kidney function
      • No retinopathy progression
    • Improved quality of life
    • Good medication adherence

    Periodic Laboratory Monitoring:

    • Frequency varies by drug:
      • ACE/ARBs, Diuretics: Cr, K+ at 2-4 weeks, then every 6-12 months
      • All patients: Annual fasting glucose, lipids
      • Diuretics: Periodic electrolytes (K+, Na+, Mg²+)
      • More frequent if abnormalities detected

    📋 Special Populations

    Pregnancy:

    • ⚠️ ACE inhibitors and ARBs are CONTRAINDICATED
      • Teratogenic - cause fetal harm
      • STOP immediately if pregnancy detected
    • Safe options in pregnancy:
      • Methyldopa (drug of choice)
      • Labetalol
      • Nifedipine (long-acting)
      • Hydralazine
    • Women of childbearing age: Discuss contraception and pregnancy plans

    Elderly:

    • Start low, go slow (more sensitive to effects)
    • Higher fall risk (orthostatic hypotension)
    • More likely to have multiple comorbidities
    • Polypharmacy concerns (drug interactions)
    • May have impaired renal/hepatic function
    • Cognitive impairment may affect adherence
    • Monitor closely for adverse effects

    Pediatric:

    • HTN in children often secondary (find cause!)
    • Dosing based on weight
    • Limited data on many antihypertensives in children
    • Lifestyle modifications important
