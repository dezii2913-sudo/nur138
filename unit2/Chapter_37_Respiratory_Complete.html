Obstructive Lung Diseases

Bronchodilators are medications used to help patients breathe by keeping airways dilated (open).

Used in Obstructive Lung Diseases:

  • Asthma: Chronic inflammation with reversible airway obstruction
  • COPD: Progressive lung disease with irreversible airway obstruction

🌬️ Asthma

What is Asthma?

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways characterized by:

  • Chronic inflammation: Airways are constantly inflamed
  • Bronchial smooth muscle spasms: Airways constrict
  • Mucus production: Excess mucus blocks airways
  • Reversible obstruction: Can be reversed with bronchodilators!

Key Feature: EXACERBATIONS or ATTACKS

During an asthma attack:

  • Airway smooth muscle contracts (bronchospasm)
  • Airways become swollen and inflamed
  • Mucus production increases
  • Result: Wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, cough

🚬 COPD (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease)

What is COPD?

COPD is a GROUP of progressive lung diseases including:

Chronic Bronchitis

Definition: Long-term inflammation of bronchial tubes

Characteristic: "Blue bloater"

Features:

  • Productive cough with mucus (≥3 months for 2+ years)
  • Airway inflammation and narrowing
  • Excess mucus production
  • Cyanosis (blue skin from low O2)
  • Edema (fluid retention)
Emphysema

Definition: Destruction and enlargement of alveoli

Characteristic: "Pink puffer"

Features:

  • Alveolar walls break down
  • Loss of elastic recoil
  • Air trapping in lungs
  • Barrel chest appearance
  • Dyspnea with minimal exertion
  • Pursed-lip breathing
💨 MEMORY: "Bronchitis = Big mucus, Emphysema = Empty alveoli" - Chronic bronchitis has mucus production, emphysema has destroyed air sacs

🚭 Main Trigger: SMOKING

⚠️ SMOKING is the #1 cause of COPD!

How smoking damages lungs:

  • Irritates and inflames airways
  • Destroys alveolar walls (emphysema)
  • Increases mucus production
  • Paralyzes cilia (tiny hairs that clear mucus)
  • Causes chronic inflammation and fibrosis

Other Risk Factors:

  • Secondhand smoke exposure
  • Air pollution
  • Occupational exposures (dust, chemicals, fumes)
  • Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (genetic)

Key Difference: Reversibility

Feature Asthma COPD
Airway Obstruction REVERSIBLE with bronchodilators IRREVERSIBLE (but can be improved)
Onset Usually childhood/young adult Usually age 40+ (after years of smoking)
Pattern Episodic attacks/exacerbations Progressive, persistent symptoms
Primary Problem Airway inflammation & spasm Airway/alveolar destruction
Main Trigger Allergens, exercise, cold air SMOKING (80-90% of cases)

🎯 How Bronchodilators Help

Bronchodilators in COPD:

Problem: In COPD, airway obstruction is IRREVERSIBLE. The airways can't completely open.

Solution: Bronchodilators can help prevent COMPLETE closure of airways during expiration!

  • Keep airways as open as possible
  • Improve air flow
  • Reduce air trapping
  • Provide mild symptomatic relief
  • Improve quality of life

💡 Important: Bronchodilators don't cure COPD, but they help patients breathe easier!

Why is COPD Irreversible?

COPD: Permanent Damage

Asthma vs COPD:

Asthma (Reversible):

  • Airways are inflamed and constricted
  • Smooth muscle spasms cause narrowing
  • Structure is intact - just needs to relax
  • Bronchodilators = airways open fully

COPD (Irreversible):

  • Airways are DESTROYED (fibrosis)
  • Alveoli are BROKEN (emphysema)
  • Scar tissue can't be "relaxed"
  • Bronchodilators = partial improvement only

💡 Think: A bent straw (asthma) can straighten. A broken straw (COPD) can't fully repair.

🔬 Pathophysiology of Asthma & COPD

💨 MEMORY: "Bronchitis = Big mucus, Emphysema = Empty alveoli" - Know the key differences!

Normal Lung Function

Normal Breathing:

  • Airways are open and clear
  • Smooth muscle relaxed
  • Minimal mucus production
  • Alveoli intact with good gas exchange
  • Elastic recoil helps expel air

Asthma Pathophysiology

What Happens in Asthma:

Step 1: Trigger Exposure

Allergens, cold air, exercise, infection → immune response

↓

Step 2: Mast Cell Degranulation

Mast cells release histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins

↓

Step 3: Triple Threat

  • Bronchospasm: Smooth muscle contracts → airways narrow
  • Inflammation: Airway walls swell
  • Mucus Hypersecretion: Excess mucus clogs airways

↓

Result: AIRWAY OBSTRUCTION

Wheezing, dyspnea, chest tightness, cough

🎯 Key Point: REVERSIBLE

With proper treatment (bronchodilators, steroids), airway structure returns to normal between attacks!

COPD Pathophysiology

Chronic Bronchitis: "Big Mucus"

Chronic irritation (usually smoking) leads to:

  • Mucus gland hyperplasia: More mucus-producing cells
  • Increased mucus production: Thick, sticky secretions
  • Cilia damage: Can't clear mucus effectively
  • Chronic inflammation: Airways constantly inflamed
  • Airway narrowing: Inflammation + mucus = obstruction

Clinical Features:

  • Productive cough (≥3 months for 2+ consecutive years)
  • Frequent respiratory infections
  • Cyanosis ("blue bloater")
  • Peripheral edema
  • Right-sided heart failure (cor pulmonale)

Emphysema: "Empty Alveoli"

Chronic inflammation leads to:

  • Protease-antiprotease imbalance: Enzymes destroy alveolar walls
  • Alveolar wall breakdown: Small sacs merge into large blebs
  • Loss of elastic recoil: Can't push air out effectively
  • Air trapping: Old air stays in lungs
  • Decreased surface area: Less gas exchange

Clinical Features:

  • Dyspnea (especially on exertion)
  • Barrel chest (hyperinflation)
  • Pursed-lip breathing
  • Weight loss (work of breathing)
  • Pink skin ("pink puffer")

Airway Obstruction in COPD

Why Airways Become IRREVERSIBLY Obstructed:

  • Fibrosis: Scar tissue forms in airway walls (can't relax)
  • Structural destruction: Alveoli permanently destroyed
  • Loss of elastic recoil: Airways collapse on expiration
  • Chronic inflammation: Ongoing damage even after stopping smoking

💡 Air Trapping:

During expiration, damaged airways collapse → air gets TRAPPED in lungs → can't exhale fully → new breath brings in less fresh air → hypoxia!

💡 Click to Expand: Why Pursed-Lip Breathing Helps

The Physics of Pursed-Lip Breathing

The Problem in COPD/Emphysema:

  • Airways lose structural support (destroyed alveoli)
  • During expiration, airways collapse prematurely
  • Air gets trapped behind collapsed airways
  • Patient can't fully exhale

How Pursed-Lip Breathing Helps:

  • Patient breathes out slowly through pursed lips (like blowing out a candle)
  • Creates BACK PRESSURE in airways
  • Back pressure keeps airways open longer
  • Allows more complete exhalation
  • Reduces air trapping
  • Improves gas exchange

🎯 Think of it like: A garden hose with a weak spot. If you put your thumb over the end (create back pressure), the hose stays rigid and doesn't collapse!

Bronchial Smooth Muscle Spasms

How Smooth Muscle Contraction Narrows Airways:

Normal: Smooth muscle around airways is relaxed → airways wide open

↓

Trigger (allergen, cold air, etc.): Immune response activated

↓

Mediator Release: Histamine, leukotrienes cause smooth muscle contraction

↓

Result: Airways constrict = BRONCHOSPASM

🎯 This is where bronchodilators work!

Bronchodilators relax bronchial smooth muscle → airways open → easier breathing

💨 Beta-2 Agonists (Bronchodilators)

🫁 MEMORY: "β2 = Breathe Better" - Beta-2 receptors in lungs cause bronchodilation

Mechanism of Action

How β2 Agonists Work:

Step 1: β2 agonist drug binds to β2 adrenergic receptors on bronchial smooth muscle

↓

Step 2: Receptor activation triggers smooth muscle RELAXATION

↓

Step 3: Airways DILATE (open wider)

↓

Result: Improved airflow, easier breathing!

Drug Classification by Duration

SHORT-Acting β2 Agonists (SABAs)

Duration: 4-6 hours

Onset: Within MINUTES (3-5 min)

Use: RESCUE inhaler for acute symptoms

Examples:

  • Albuterol (ProAir, Ventolin, Proventil): Most common SABA
  • Levalbuterol (Xopenex): R-isomer of albuterol

When to Use:

  • Acute asthma attack
  • Pre-exercise (exercise-induced asthma)
  • PRN for shortness of breath
  • Before inhaled steroids (opens airways first)
LONG-Acting β2 Agonists (LABAs)

Duration: 12-24 hours

Onset: SLOWER (15-30 min)

Use: MAINTENANCE therapy (NOT rescue)

Examples:

  • Salmeterol (Serevent): Twice daily (12 hrs)
  • Formoterol (Foradil): Twice daily (12 hrs)
  • Arformoterol (Brovana): Nebulizer solution

When to Use:

  • Long-term asthma control
  • COPD maintenance
  • Prevention of nocturnal symptoms
  • MUST be combined with inhaled corticosteroid in asthma

🚨 BLACK BOX WARNING for LABAs:

NEVER use LABAs as monotherapy for asthma!

  • Increased risk of asthma-related DEATH when used alone
  • MUST be combined with inhaled corticosteroid
  • Okay to use alone in COPD (different pathophysiology)
⚡ MEMORY: "SABA for SAVE, LABA for LONG-term" - Short-acting saves lives in attacks, Long-acting for daily control

Common Combination Products

LABA + Inhaled Corticosteroid Combinations:

Brand Name LABA Steroid
Advair Salmeterol Fluticasone
Symbicort Formoterol Budesonide
Dulera Formoterol Mometasone
Breo Ellipta Vilanterol Fluticasone

β2 Receptor Selectivity

Why β2 Selectivity Matters:

β2 receptors: Located in LUNGS → bronchodilation (what we want!)

β1 receptors: Located in HEART → increased heart rate, contractility (side effects!)

Modern β2 agonists are β2-SELECTIVE, meaning they preferentially target lung receptors. However, at high doses, they can still affect β1 receptors in the heart.

🚫 Anticholinergics (Antimuscarinics)

🚫 MEMORY: "Anti-ACh = Anti-Constriction" - Block acetylcholine to prevent bronchoconstriction

Mechanism of Action

How Anticholinergics Work:

Normal Physiology:

Parasympathetic nervous system releases acetylcholine (ACh) → binds to muscarinic receptors on bronchial smooth muscle → BRONCHOCONSTRICTION

With Anticholinergic Drug:

Step 1: Anticholinergic drug BLOCKS muscarinic receptors

↓

Step 2: Acetylcholine can't bind

↓

Step 3: Bronchoconstriction prevented

↓

Result: Airways stay OPEN (bronchodilation)

Drug Examples

SHORT-Acting: Ipratropium (Atrovent)

Duration: 6-8 hours

Onset: 15-30 minutes

Route: Inhaled (MDI or nebulizer)

Uses:

  • COPD maintenance (first-line)
  • Acute asthma exacerbations (with albuterol)
  • Chronic bronchitis

Note: Often combined with albuterol (Combivent, DuoNeb)

LONG-Acting: Tiotropium (Spiriva)

Duration: 24 hours (once daily!)

Onset: Gradual (peak at 1-3 hours)

Route: Inhaled (HandiHaler, Respimat)

Uses:

  • COPD maintenance therapy
  • Long-term asthma control
  • Once-daily dosing improves compliance

Advantage: Very long duration = better adherence

Special Use in COPD

🎯 Anticholinergics are OFTEN FIRST-LINE for COPD!

Why?

  • COPD patients have increased vagal tone (more acetylcholine activity)
  • Blocking ACh is very effective in COPD
  • Often combined with LABAs for synergistic effect
  • Can be used as monotherapy (unlike LABAs in asthma)

Common Combination: Anticholinergic + LABA

Brand Name Anticholinergic LABA
Combivent/DuoNeb Ipratropium (short-acting) Albuterol (short-acting)
Anoro Ellipta Umeclidinium (long-acting) Vilanterol (long-acting)
Stiolto Respimat Tiotropium (long-acting) Olodaterol (long-acting)
💨 MEMORY: "Ipra = Short (hours), Tio = Long (24)" - Ipratropium lasts hours, Tiotropium lasts a day

☕ Methylxanthines

⚠️ MEMORY: "Methyl = Many side effects" - Narrow therapeutic window with lots of adverse effects

What Are Methylxanthines?

Methylxanthines are bronchodilators that are chemically related to caffeine!

Primary Drug: Theophylline

Status: LESS commonly used today due to side effects and narrow therapeutic window

Mechanism of Action

Multiple mechanisms (not fully understood):

  • Phosphodiesterase inhibition: Increases cAMP → smooth muscle relaxation
  • Adenosine receptor antagonism: Blocks bronchoconstriction
  • Anti-inflammatory effects: Reduces airway inflammation
  • Respiratory stimulant: Stimulates respiratory drive

⚠️ VERY Narrow Therapeutic Window

🚨 Critical Concept: Narrow Therapeutic Window

Therapeutic Range: 5-15 mcg/mL (some sources say 10-20)

Toxic Level: >20 mcg/mL

Why This Matters:

  • Small increase in dose = TOXICITY
  • Requires frequent monitoring of blood levels
  • Many drug-drug interactions
  • Variable metabolism between patients

Side Effects (LOTS!)

Common Side Effects:

  • GI: Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, GERD
  • CNS: Insomnia, restlessness, headache, irritability
  • Cardiovascular: Tachycardia, palpitations, arrhythmias
  • Other: Tremors, diuresis (increased urination)

🚨 Severe/Toxic Effects:

  • Seizures (can occur WITHOUT warning!)
  • Serious arrhythmias (ventricular tachycardia)
  • Hypotension
  • Death

💡 Seizures can be the FIRST sign of toxicity - no prior warning symptoms!

Drug Interactions (Many!)

Theophylline is metabolized by CYP1A2 (different from CYP3A4)

Interaction Effect Examples
CYP1A2 Inhibitors ↑ Theophylline levels → TOXICITY Ciprofloxacin, erythromycin, cimetidine
CYP1A2 Inducers ↓ Theophylline levels → Ineffective Smoking, phenytoin, rifampin
Beta-blockers Antagonize bronchodilation Propranolol, metoprolol

🚬 SMOKING significantly affects theophylline!

Smoking INDUCES CYP1A2 → INCREASES theophylline metabolism → LOWER drug levels

Important: If patient quits smoking, theophylline levels will RISE (dose must be reduced!)

Why Methylxanthines Are Less Popular Now

Reasons theophylline is rarely first-line:

  • ✗ Narrow therapeutic window (hard to dose)
  • ✗ MANY side effects
  • ✗ Requires frequent monitoring (blood levels)
  • ✗ MANY drug interactions
  • ✗ Risk of seizures and arrhythmias
  • ✗ Safer alternatives available (β2 agonists, anticholinergics)

When Theophylline Might Still Be Used:

  • Patient doesn't respond to standard bronchodilators
  • Cost consideration (it's inexpensive)
  • Add-on therapy for severe COPD
  • Some patients prefer oral medication over inhalers

⚠️ Side Effects & Cautions

💓 MEMORY: "Shake & Race = β2 side effects" - Tremors (shake) and tachycardia (racing heart) from beta-agonists

Beta-2 Agonist Side Effects

Common Side Effects:

  • Tremors: Fine hand tremors (dose-related)
  • Tachycardia: Increased heart rate (β1 stimulation at high doses)
  • Palpitations: Feeling heart racing or pounding
  • Nervousness/Restlessness: CNS stimulation
  • Insomnia: Difficulty sleeping (avoid evening doses)
  • Headache: Common, usually mild
  • Hypokalemia: Low potassium (shifts K+ into cells)
  • Hyperglycemia: Elevated blood sugar (stimulates glycogenolysis)

🚨 Serious Side Effects (High Doses or Overuse):

  • Cardiac arrhythmias: Atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia
  • Myocardial ischemia: Chest pain, especially in patients with CAD
  • Heart failure exacerbation: In susceptible patients
  • DEATH: From cardiac events (especially with excessive SABA use)

⚠️ Excessive SABA use (>2 canisters/month) indicates poor asthma control and increases risk of death!

Use with Caution In:

Condition Why Caution Needed
Cardiovascular Disease Tachycardia and arrhythmias can worsen angina or trigger MI
Diabetes Can raise blood glucose levels
Hyperthyroidism Already have tachycardia; β2 agonists worsen it
Hypertension Can increase blood pressure
Seizure Disorders CNS stimulation may lower seizure threshold
Elderly Patients More sensitive to cardiac and CNS effects

Anticholinergic Side Effects

Classic "Anticholinergic Effects":

Remember: Anticholinergics BLOCK parasympathetic (rest & digest) activity

  • Dry mouth: Decreased saliva production
  • Dry eyes: Decreased tear production
  • Blurred vision: Pupil dilation (mydriasis)
  • Urinary retention: Difficulty urinating (especially in older men)
  • Constipation: Decreased GI motility
  • Tachycardia: Unopposed sympathetic activity

⚠️ Use Anticholinergics with CAUTION in:

  • Narrow-angle glaucoma: Pupil dilation increases intraocular pressure → can cause acute glaucoma attack
  • Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH): Worsens urinary retention
  • Urinary retention: Will worsen condition
  • Tachyarrhythmias: Can increase heart rate
👁️ MEMORY: "Can't see, can't pee, can't spit, can't shit" - Classic anticholinergic side effects (blurred vision, urinary retention, dry mouth, constipation)

Contraindications

DO NOT USE Beta-2 Agonists if:

  • Hypersensitivity/allergy to the drug
  • Uncontrolled tachyarrhythmias
  • Severe untreated hyperthyroidism

DO NOT USE Anticholinergics if:

  • Hypersensitivity to atropine or derivatives
  • Acute narrow-angle glaucoma
  • Severe urinary retention or bladder neck obstruction

🛡️ Mast Cell Stabilizers & Leukotriene Modifiers

🛡️ MEMORY: "Stabilize = Stop the attack before it starts" - Preventive therapy, NOT for acute attacks

Mast Cell Stabilizers

Primary Drug: Cromolyn Sodium (Intal)

Mechanism of Action:

Step 1: Prevent mast cell degranulation

↓

Step 2: Block release of histamine and other inflammatory mediators

↓

Result: Prevent asthma attack from starting!

Route: Inhaled (nebulizer solution or MDI)

Use: MAINTENANCE therapy for asthma (prophylaxis)

Onset: SLOW - takes 2-4 weeks for full effect

⚠️ CRITICAL TEACHING POINT:

Cromolyn is for PREVENTION ONLY - NOT for acute asthma attacks!

  • Must be used regularly (even when feeling well)
  • Takes weeks to work
  • Does NOT provide immediate relief
  • Keep using SABA (albuterol) for rescue

Advantages of Cromolyn:

  • ✓ Very SAFE - minimal side effects
  • ✓ Safe for children
  • ✓ Safe in pregnancy
  • ✓ No systemic effects
  • ✓ Works locally in lungs

Leukotriene Modifiers

Primary Drug: Montelukast (Singulair)

Class: Leukotriene Receptor Antagonist (LTRA)

Mechanism of Action:

What are Leukotrienes? Inflammatory mediators that cause:

  • Bronchoconstriction
  • Increased mucus production
  • Airway inflammation and edema

How Montelukast Works:

Blocks leukotriene receptors → prevents inflammatory response → reduces asthma symptoms

Route: ORAL (tablet or chewable)

Dosing: Once daily (usually at bedtime)

Use: Maintenance therapy for asthma, especially ASPIRIN-INDUCED asthma

Special Use: Aspirin-Induced Asthma

Some patients develop asthma attacks after taking aspirin or NSAIDs

Why? Aspirin blocks COX → shunts arachidonic acid pathway toward LEUKOTRIENE production

Solution: Leukotriene modifiers are especially effective for these patients!

Side Effects

Cromolyn Side Effects (Minimal!):

  • Throat irritation (from inhalation)
  • Cough
  • Unpleasant taste
  • Rare: bronchospasm (use bronchodilator first)

Montelukast (Singulair) - BLACK BOX WARNING:

🚨 Neuropsychiatric Events:

  • Agitation, aggression, hostility
  • Anxiety, depression
  • Suicidal thoughts and behavior
  • Sleep disturbances, vivid dreams/nightmares

💡 Nursing Action: Monitor for mood/behavior changes, especially in children and adolescents. Discontinue if psychiatric symptoms develop.

Advantages of Montelukast:

  • ✓ ORAL administration (no inhaler needed!)
  • ✓ Once-daily dosing
  • ✓ Good for patients who struggle with inhalers
  • ✓ Effective for exercise-induced asthma
  • ✓ Safe in pregnancy (Category B)

Nursing Considerations

Patient Education:

  • Use even when feeling well: These are maintenance medications
  • Takes time to work: Not for acute symptoms
  • Continue SABA: Keep rescue inhaler for attacks
  • Monitor for side effects: Especially mood changes with montelukast
  • Liver function tests: Baseline and periodic monitoring for montelukast

💊 Corticosteroids

🔥 MEMORY: "Steroids = Stamp out inflammation" - Corticosteroids reduce airway inflammation

Why Corticosteroids for Asthma?

Asthma is fundamentally an INFLAMMATORY disease!

Bronchodilators open airways, but corticosteroids address the ROOT CAUSE: inflammation

Mechanism of Action

How Corticosteroids Work:

"Put out the fire of an immune response"

  • Reduce inflammation: Decrease production of inflammatory mediators
  • Stabilize cell membranes: Prevent mast cell degranulation
  • Reduce airway edema: Decrease swelling
  • Decrease mucus production: Less airway obstruction
  • Enhance β2 receptor function: Make beta-agonists work BETTER!

Types of Corticosteroids

INHALED Corticosteroids (ICS)

Use: Long-term control of PERSISTENT asthma

Route: Inhaled (MDI, DPI, or nebulizer)

Advantages: Local action, minimal systemic effects

Examples:

  • Fluticasone (Flovent): Common ICS
  • Budesonide (Pulmicort): Available as nebulizer solution
  • Beclomethasone (QVAR): Older ICS
  • Mometasone (Asmanex): Once-daily DPI

💡 First-line for persistent asthma!

SYSTEMIC Corticosteroids

Use: Acute asthma exacerbations

Route: IV or PO (oral)

Disadvantage: Systemic side effects

Examples:

  • Prednisone (PO): Oral steroid burst
  • Methylprednisolone (IV): For severe exacerbations in hospital
  • Prednisolone (PO): Liquid formulation for children

Typical Course: 3-10 days ("steroid burst")

⚠️ Corticosteroids are NOT for SOLE therapy of acute asthma attacks!

They work too slowly. Always use bronchodilators (SABA) FIRST for immediate relief!

Steroids take hours to work - not immediate like bronchodilators

Adverse Effects

INHALED Corticosteroids (Usually Well-Tolerated):

  • Oral candidiasis (thrush): Most common - fungal infection in mouth
  • Hoarseness/dysphonia: Voice changes
  • Cough: From local irritation
  • Minimal systemic effects at normal doses

💡 PREVENTION of Oral Candidiasis:

RINSE MOUTH after each use!

  • Swish and spit water
  • Removes steroid residue from mouth/throat
  • Prevents fungal overgrowth
  • Use spacer device to reduce oral deposition

SYSTEMIC Corticosteroids (Many Side Effects!):

Short-term use (days to weeks):

  • Hyperglycemia (increased blood sugar)
  • Increased appetite and weight gain
  • Mood changes (euphoria, insomnia, irritability)
  • Fluid retention
  • Hypertension

Long-term use (months):

  • Cushing's syndrome (moon face, buffalo hump)
  • Osteoporosis (bone loss)
  • Immunosuppression (increased infection risk)
  • Adrenal suppression
  • Cataracts and glaucoma
  • Growth suppression in children
  • Skin changes (thinning, bruising, striae)

Drug Interactions

⚠️ Important Interactions:

  • Antidiabetic drugs: Steroids INCREASE blood glucose
  • NSAIDs: Increased risk of GI ulcers when combined
  • Diuretics: Steroids cause fluid retention (opposing effects)
  • CYP3A4 inducers/inhibitors: Affect steroid metabolism

Nursing Considerations

📋 Important Teaching Points:

  • Rinse mouth after inhaled steroids: Prevent thrush
  • Use spacer device: Improves delivery, reduces oral deposition
  • Use even when feeling well: Maintenance therapy
  • Takes time to work: Not for acute attacks
  • Never stop systemic steroids abruptly: Must taper to avoid adrenal crisis
  • Monitor blood glucose: Especially in diabetics
  • Watch for signs of infection: Immunosuppression
  • Bone health: Calcium/Vitamin D supplementation for long-term use
🦷 MEMORY: "Rinse after steroids, breathe after bronchodilators" - Always rinse mouth after inhaled corticosteroids to prevent thrush

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care & Patient Education

💨 MEMORY: "SBIR = Shake, Breathe In, Hold, Rinse" - MDI technique steps

🫁 MDI (Metered Dose Inhaler) Technique

Proper MDI Use - Step by Step:

1. SHAKE the inhaler

  • Shake vigorously for 5 seconds
  • Mixes medication evenly

2. EXHALE completely

  • Breathe out fully to empty lungs
  • Makes room for medication

3. Position inhaler correctly

  • Open-mouth technique (preferred): Hold inhaler 1-2 inches from open mouth
  • Closed-mouth technique: Place mouthpiece between lips (seal tightly)
  • With spacer (best!): Attach spacer, seal lips around mouthpiece

4. BREATHE IN slowly and deeply

  • Press down on canister ONCE
  • Begin inhaling JUST BEFORE pressing
  • Continue slow, deep breath (3-5 seconds)

5. HOLD breath for 10 seconds

  • Allows medication to deposit in airways
  • Count slowly: "1-one-thousand, 2-one-thousand..."

6. Exhale slowly

  • Breathe out through nose or pursed lips

7. Wait 1 minute between puffs

  • If prescribed multiple puffs
  • Allows first dose to work

8. RINSE mouth (if corticosteroid)

  • Swish and spit water
  • Prevents thrush

🔄 Click to Learn:

Why Use a Spacer?

Benefits of Spacer Device

Problems Without Spacer:

  • Requires perfect coordination (press + breathe)
  • Much medication hits back of throat
  • Less medication reaches lungs
  • More oral side effects (thrush from steroids)

With Spacer:

  • ✓ Holds medication in chamber
  • ✓ No timing needed
  • ✓ More medication to lungs
  • ✓ Less oral deposition
  • ✓ Especially helpful for children & elderly

💡 Spacers improve drug delivery by 20-30%!

Bronchodilator Use Order

When Using Multiple Inhalers:

ALWAYS use bronchodilator FIRST, then corticosteroid!

Step 1: Use SABA (albuterol) first

↓

Step 2: Wait 5-10 minutes

↓

Step 3: Airways now OPEN from bronchodilator

↓

Step 4: Use inhaled corticosteroid

↓

Result: Steroid reaches deeper into open airways!

💨 MEMORY: "Open first, treat second" - Bronchodilator opens airways, then steroid treats inflammation

Inhaler Cleaning & Maintenance

Patient Education:

  • Clean inhaler weekly: Remove canister, rinse plastic holder with warm water, air dry
  • Check doses remaining: Many inhalers have counters
  • Don't immerse canister in water: Damages medication
  • Store at room temperature: Not too hot or cold
  • Keep track of refills: Don't run out during exacerbation

Assessment & Monitoring

Baseline Assessment:

  • Respiratory status: Rate, depth, effort, breath sounds
  • Oxygen saturation: Pulse oximetry
  • Peak flow measurement: Baseline lung function
  • Medication history: Current use, allergies
  • Trigger identification: Allergens, exercise, cold air
  • Smoking history: Current or past use
  • Comorbidities: Cardiac disease, diabetes

Ongoing Monitoring:

  • Respiratory assessment: Before and after treatments
  • Vital signs: Especially HR (tachycardia from β2 agonists)
  • O2 saturation: Trending over time
  • Peak flow readings: Track lung function
  • SABA use frequency: >2 canisters/month = poor control
  • Side effects: Tremors, palpitations, mood changes
  • Blood glucose: If on systemic steroids

Patient Education - Key Points

📚 Critical Teaching:

Understanding Medication Purpose:

  • SABA (albuterol): Rescue inhaler - use for acute symptoms
  • LABA: Long-term control - NOT for rescue
  • Inhaled steroids: Daily maintenance - reduce inflammation
  • Anticholinergics: Daily maintenance - keep airways open

When to Seek Help:

  • SABA not working or needed more frequently
  • Peak flow in RED zone (<50% personal best)
  • Severe dyspnea, chest pain, cyanosis
  • Cannot speak in full sentences
  • Confusion or drowsiness (severe hypoxia)

Lifestyle Modifications:

  • Avoid triggers: Allergens, smoke, cold air, pollution
  • Quit smoking: Absolutely essential for COPD
  • Get vaccinations: Annual flu shot, pneumonia vaccine
  • Exercise regularly: Improves lung function (use pre-exercise inhaler)
  • Maintain healthy weight: Reduces work of breathing

Liver Function Tests

⚠️ Monitor LFTs for:

  • Montelukast (Singulair): Can cause hepatotoxicity
  • Baseline and periodic monitoring
  • Discontinue if significant elevation

Special Populations

Children:

  • Use spacer with mask for young children
  • Nebulizers often easier than MDI
  • Monitor growth (steroids can suppress)
  • Teach parents AND child proper technique

Elderly:

  • May need extra help coordinating MDI
  • Consider DPI (dry powder inhaler) - breath-activated
  • More sensitive to cardiovascular side effects
  • Check vision for proper inhaler use

Pregnant Patients:

  • Asthma control critical for fetal oxygenation
  • Most inhaled medications safe in pregnancy
  • Budesonide is Category B (safest ICS)
  • Benefits usually outweigh risks
