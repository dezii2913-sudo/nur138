🫀 Cardiovascular Medications High-yield for exam

```
🩸 Hypertension Definitions & Stages (ACC/AHA Guidelines)
🎯 EXAM FOCUS: Know the exact numbers for each stage. Questions often ask you to classify BP readings.
CategorySystolic (mmHg)Diastolic (mmHg)Management
Normal <120 AND <80 Promote lifestyle modifications for prevention
Elevated 120–129 AND <80 Lifestyle changes + reassess in 3-6 months
Stage 1 HTN 130–139 OR 80–89 Lifestyle + medication if high CV risk
Stage 2 HTN ≥140 OR ≥90 Lifestyle + medication (usually 2 drugs)
Hypertensive Crisis ≥180 AND/OR ≥120 ⚠️ Emergency if symptoms present (chest pain, SOB, neuro changes)
💡 Memory Tip: "130/80 = Stage 1, 140/90 = Stage 2" — Remember these thresholds!
🔄 RAAS System (Why ACE Inhibitors & ARBs Work)
The Cascade:
1. Kidney senses low BP → releases RENIN
2. Renin converts angiotensinogen → Angiotensin I
3. ACE (in lungs) converts Angiotensin I → Angiotensin II
4. Angiotensin II: VASOCONSTRICTION + stimulates ALDOSTERONE
5. Aldosterone: Retains Na⁺ & H₂O (↑BP) and Dumps K⁺ (↓K⁺)
💊 How Drugs Interrupt:
• ACE Inhibitors (-pril): Block ACE → ↓ Ang II formation → ↓BP, ↓aldosterone
• ARBs (-sartan): Block Ang II receptors → same BP effect, different mechanism
• Aldosterone Antagonists: Block aldosterone → retain K⁺, lose Na⁺/H₂O
💊 ACE Inhibitors (-pril)
⚠️ KEY ADVERSE EFFECTS:
  • Dry cough — #1 most common (ACE breakdown causes bradykinin accumulation in lungs)
  • Angioedema — LIFE-THREATENING swelling of face/lips/tongue/throat → STOP drug immediately, assess airway
  • Hyperkalemia (↑K⁺) — ↓aldosterone → body retains K⁺
  • Acute kidney injury — especially with bilateral renal artery stenosis
🚫 CONTRAINDICATIONS: Pregnancy (teratogenic), bilateral renal artery stenosis, history of angioedema, hyperkalemia
📋 NURSING IMPLICATIONS:
• Monitor: BP, K⁺ levels, BUN/Cr (renal function)
• Teach: Change positions slowly (orthostatic hypotension), report facial swelling immediately
• Hold: If BP drops below parameter (typically <100 systolic) or K⁺ >5.5
• Patient teaching: "Report any swelling of your face, lips, or tongue immediately — this is an emergency"
💡 Memory: "ACE = Accumulated Cough & Edema" — The two big side effects!
💊 ARBs (-sartan)
✅ ADVANTAGES over ACE Inhibitors:
• NO cough (doesn't affect bradykinin)
• NO angioedema (or very rare)
• Same BP-lowering effect
• Better tolerated overall
⚠️ STILL CAUSES:
• Hyperkalemia (↑K⁺) — Monitor K⁺ levels
• Renal dysfunction — Monitor BUN/Cr
• Hypotension — First-dose effect possible
🚫 CONTRAINDICATIONS: Pregnancy (teratogenic — same as ACE-I), bilateral renal artery stenosis
📋 NURSING: Same monitoring as ACE-I (BP, K⁺, renal function), but ARBs are often used when patient can't tolerate ACE-I due to cough
💊 Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)

Non-Dihydropyridines

Diltiazem, Verapamil

Effects: ↓HR, ↓AV conduction, ↓contractility

Uses: HTN, angina, A-fib rate control

Watch for: Bradycardia, heart block, HF exacerbation

Dihydropyridines

Amlodipine, Nifedipine

Effects: Vasodilation (minimal cardiac effects)

Uses: HTN, angina

Watch for: Peripheral edema, flushing, headache

⚠️ GRAPEFRUIT JUICE INTERACTION: Increases CCB levels → ↑hypotension, ↑adverse effects. Avoid grapefruit juice!
📋 NURSING:
• Monitor: HR and BP before each dose
• Hold parameters: HR <60 (for diltiazem/verapamil), BP <100 systolic
• Teach: Avoid grapefruit juice, change positions slowly, report swelling of ankles
💊 Beta Blockers
🎯 Mechanism: Block beta-adrenergic receptors
• β1 (heart): ↓HR, ↓contractility, ↓renin release → ↓BP
• β2 (lungs, vessels): Can cause bronchoconstriction if blocked

β1-Selective (Cardioselective)

Metoprolol, Atenolol, Bisoprolol

✅ Safer in asthma/COPD (but still use caution!)

Primarily affect heart, less bronchospasm risk

Non-Selective

Propranolol, Nadolol

❌ AVOID in asthma/COPD

Block both β1 and β2 → can trigger severe bronchospasm

🚫 CRITICAL SAFETY POINTS:
• Do NOT stop abruptly → Rebound HTN, tachycardia, MI risk. Must taper!
• Masks hypoglycemia symptoms in diabetics → Blocks tachycardia/tremor; can still sweat
• Can worsen heart failure → Negative inotrope (↓contractility)
• Bradycardia & heart block risk → Don't combine with other rate-lowering drugs
📋 NURSING IMPLICATIONS:
• Monitor: HR and BP before each dose; Hold if HR <60 or BP <100 systolic (typical parameters)
• Diabetic patients: Teach to monitor blood glucose closely; may not feel usual hypo symptoms
• Assess: Lung sounds (bronchospasm), peripheral circulation (cold extremities)
• Patient teaching: "Take your pulse daily. Don't stop this medication suddenly — call if you want to stop"
💡 Memory: "Beta 1 = 1 heart, Beta 2 = 2 lungs" — β1 receptors in heart, β2 receptors in lungs!
💊 Diuretics
General principle: ↓fluid volume → ↓BP
Different types work at different parts of the nephron
TypeExamplesK⁺ EffectKey Points
Loop Diuretics Furosemide (Lasix), Bumetanide ↓K⁺ Most potent; IV/PO; ototoxicity risk; rapid diuresis
Thiazides Hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ), Chlorthalidone ↓K⁺ First-line for HTN; less potent than loop; photosensitivity
K⁺-Sparing Spironolactone, Amiloride ↑K⁺ Weak diuretic; often combined with HCTZ; monitor K⁺!
⚠️ HYPOKALEMIA SIGNS (Loop & Thiazide):
Muscle cramps, weakness, fatigue, cardiac dysrhythmias, constipation
→ Monitor K⁺ levels, encourage K⁺-rich foods, may need supplement
⚠️ HYPERKALEMIA RISK (K⁺-Sparing):
Especially dangerous when combined with ACE-I or ARBs!
→ Monitor K⁺ closely; avoid salt substitutes (high in K⁺)
📋 NURSING IMPLICATIONS:
• Monitor: Daily weights, I&Os, BP, electrolytes (especially K⁺), BUN/Cr
• Teach: Take in morning (avoid nocturia), change positions slowly (orthostatic hypotension)
• Loop diuretics: Monitor hearing (ototoxicity), avoid with aminoglycosides (↑ototoxicity)
• Thiazides: Teach sun protection (photosensitivity), may worsen gout (↑uric acid)
💡 Memory: "Loop Loses K⁺, K-Sparing Keeps K⁺"
```

⚡ Autonomic Nervous System Know this cold for exam

```
🧠 SNS vs PNS Overview

Sympathetic (SNS) — "Fight or Flight"

Neurotransmitter: Norepinephrine (NE), Epinephrine

Effects:

  • ↑HR, ↑BP, ↑contractility
  • Bronchodilation (β2)
  • ↓GI motility
  • Urinary retention
  • Mydriasis (pupil dilation)
  • ↑Blood glucose

Parasympathetic (PNS) — "Rest & Digest"

Neurotransmitter: Acetylcholine (ACh)

Effects:

  • ↓HR, ↓BP
  • Bronchoconstriction
  • ↑GI motility & secretions
  • Urination
  • Miosis (pupil constriction)
  • ↑Secretions (saliva, tears, sweat)
💡 Memory:
Cholinergic (PNS) = "WET" — Sweating, Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation
Anticholinergic = "DRY & FAST" — Dry mouth, Tachycardia, Hot, Constipation, Urinary retention
💊 Atropine (Anticholinergic)
MOA: Blocks muscarinic receptors → Inhibits parasympathetic (ACh) effects
Clinical Uses:
• Bradycardia — Increases HR by blocking vagal (PNS) effects on heart
• Organophosphate poisoning — Reverses excessive cholinergic stimulation
• Preanesthetic — Decreases secretions during surgery
• Ophthalmic — Mydriasis for eye exams
⚠️ Anticholinergic Effects ("Hot, Dry, Blind, Fast, Constipated"):
• Tachycardia
• Dry mouth, dry skin, ↓sweating (heat intolerance!)
• Mydriasis (dilated pupils), blurred vision
• Urinary retention
• Constipation
• Confusion (especially elderly)
🚫 CONTRAINDICATIONS:
• Narrow-angle glaucoma — Mydriasis ↑intraocular pressure → blindness risk
• BPH/urinary retention — Will worsen urinary retention
• Tachyarrhythmias — Will increase HR further
• Hyperthyroidism — Can precipitate thyroid storm
📋 NURSING:
• Monitor: HR (target ↑HR if given for bradycardia), pupil size, urinary output
• Elderly: High risk for confusion, falls, urinary retention
• Teach: Avoid overheating (↓sweating), report difficulty urinating, use sunglasses (photophobia from mydriasis)
💡 Toxicity Memory: "Mad as a hatter, blind as a bat, red as a beet, hot as a hare, dry as a bone"
💊 Epinephrine (Sympathomimetic)
MOA: Stimulates ALL adrenergic receptors (α1, α2, β1, β2)
• β1: ↑HR, ↑contractility, ↑cardiac output
• β2: Bronchodilation, vasodilation (skeletal muscle)
• α1: Vasoconstriction (skin, mucosa, viscera)
🚨 PRIMARY USE: ANAPHYLAXIS
First-line emergency treatment for anaphylactic shock
Route: IM (preferred) into anterolateral thigh
Dose: 0.3-0.5 mg (1:1000 concentration)
May repeat q5-15min if no response
Other Uses:
• Cardiac arrest (ACLS) — IV/IO route
• Severe asthma/bronchospasm (when other treatments fail)
• Combined with local anesthetics (vasoconstriction → ↑duration, ↓bleeding)
⚠️ ADVERSE EFFECTS:
• Tachycardia, palpitations, arrhythmias
• Hypertension
• Tremor, anxiety, nervousness
• Hyperglycemia (stimulates glycogenolysis)
📋 NURSING — Anaphylaxis:
1. Assess airway — Priority! Stridor = impending obstruction
2. Give epinephrine IM immediately — Don't delay!
3. Call for help / activate emergency response
4. Position patient supine with legs elevated (if no respiratory distress)
5. Give O₂, establish IV access
6. Monitor continuously: VS (especially BP and HR), respiratory status, ECG
7. Be prepared to repeat epinephrine q5-15min if needed
8. Adjunct treatments: IV fluids, H1/H2 blockers, corticosteroids, albuterol
💡 Anaphylaxis Memory: "Epi First, IM Thigh, Don't Die" — Epinephrine is FIRST-LINE, given IM in anterolateral thigh
🎯 Beta-1 vs Beta-2 Receptor Effects
ReceptorLocationStimulation EffectBlockade Effect
β1 (Beta-1) Heart ✅ ↑HR
✅ ↑Contractility
✅ ↑AV conduction 		❌ ↓HR (Bradycardia)
❌ ↓Contractility
❌ Heart block risk
β2 (Beta-2) Lungs, Blood vessels, Uterus ✅ Bronchodilation
✅ Vasodilation (skeletal muscle)
✅ ↑Glycogenolysis → ↑glucose
✅ ↓K⁺ (shifts into cells) 		❌ Bronchoconstriction
❌ Can trigger asthma attack
🎯 EXAM APPLICATION:
• Albuterol (β2 agonist): Bronchodilation for asthma; watch for tachycardia (also stimulates β1) and ↓K⁺
• Metoprolol (β1-selective blocker): ↓HR and BP; relatively safer in asthma (doesn't block β2 as much)
• Propranolol (non-selective β-blocker): Blocks BOTH β1 and β2 → AVOID in asthma/COPD (bronchospasm risk)
💡 Memory: "β1 = 1 heart, β2 = 2 lungs"
```

🦠 Antibiotics & Infection Control High-yield

```
📚 Key Definitions (Infection, Colonization, Disinfectant vs Antiseptic)
TermDefinitionClinical Example
Colonization Microorganisms are PRESENT on/in body but NOT causing disease or symptoms. No immune response. MRSA in nares without infection. Does NOT require treatment.
Infection Microorganisms have INVADED tissue, multiplying, triggering immune response. Signs/symptoms present. MRSA wound infection: redness, warmth, purulent drainage, fever. Requires antibiotics.
Disease Infection causing clear signs and symptoms; patient is clinically ill. Patient with pneumonia: fever, cough, infiltrates on CXR, elevated WBC
Antiseptic Chemical that INHIBITS growth of microorganisms on LIVING TISSUE. Bacteriostatic (doesn't kill). Hand sanitizer, Betadine on skin before surgery
Disinfectant Chemical that KILLS microorganisms on NON-LIVING objects/surfaces. Bactericidal. Bleach on countertops, instrument sterilization solutions
💡 Memory:
Antiseptic = Living tissue (hand sanitizer on YOUR hands)
Disinfectant = Dead objects (bleach on TABLES)
💊 Vancomycin — MRSA Drug of Choice
MOA: Inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis
Coverage: Gram-POSITIVE only (MRSA, VRE, C. difficile)
Uses:
• IV vancomycin: MRSA infections (bacteremia, pneumonia, skin/soft tissue)
• Oral vancomycin: C. difficile colitis ONLY (not absorbed systemically)
🚨 RED MAN SYNDROME — KNOW THIS!
Cause: Infusing TOO FAST → Rapid histamine release
Signs:
• Flushing/redness of face, neck, upper chest ("red man")
• Pruritus (itching)
• HYPOTENSION (NOT hypertension!)
• Rash, possibly tachycardia
Action:
1. STOP the infusion immediately
2. Give antihistamine (diphenhydramine/Benadryl) as ordered
3. Wait for symptoms to resolve
4. Restart at SLOWER rate (≥60 minutes per gram)
KEY: This is NOT a true allergy! Patient CAN receive vancomycin again — just infuse slower!
⚠️ TOXICITIES (Same as Aminoglycosides):
• NEPHROTOXICITY — Monitor BUN/Cr, I&Os
• OTOTOXICITY — Monitor hearing, assess for tinnitus, vertigo
📋 NURSING IMPLICATIONS:
• Infusion rate: MUST infuse over ≥60 minutes (1 gram per hour maximum)
• Monitor trough levels: Draw right before 4th or 5th dose (goal 10-20 mcg/mL)
• Monitor: BUN/Cr (nephrotoxicity), hearing (ototoxicity), IV site (phlebitis/extravasation)
• Avoid combining with aminoglycosides if possible (additive nephro/ototoxicity)
• C. diff: Oral vancomycin stays in GI tract; IV vancomycin does NOT treat C. diff
💡 Memory: "Vanc goes SLOW or you'll be RED from head to toe" — Red Man Syndrome from fast infusion
💊 Tetracyclines (Doxycycline)
MOA: Inhibit bacterial protein synthesis (bind to 30S ribosomal subunit)
Coverage: Broad-spectrum (many Gram+, Gram-, atypicals like Chlamydia, Rickettsia)
Common Uses:
• Acne
• Lyme disease (doxycycline is first-line)
• Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever
• Chlamydia, Mycoplasma pneumonia
• Community-acquired MRSA skin infections
• Malaria prophylaxis (doxycycline)
🚫 CRITICAL RESTRICTIONS (HIGH-YIELD):
1. NO dairy, antacids, or iron supplements
→ Chelation (binds to Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺) → Drug becomes inactive
→ Take 1 hour BEFORE or 2 hours AFTER these products
2. NO in pregnancy
→ Crosses placenta → Permanent YELLOW-BROWN tooth discoloration in fetus
3. NO in children <8 years
→ Binds to developing teeth → Permanent tooth staining and enamel hypoplasia
4. PHOTOSENSITIVITY
→ Severe sunburn risk → Teach: sunscreen, protective clothing, avoid tanning beds
⚠️ OTHER ADVERSE EFFECTS:
• Esophagitis/esophageal ulceration — Take with FULL glass of water, stay UPRIGHT ≥30 minutes
• GI upset (N/V/D) — Take with food (doxycycline can be taken with food, unlike other tetracyclines)
• Superinfection risk — Broad spectrum kills normal flora → C. diff, yeast
📋 NURSING:
• Before administering: Verify NOT pregnant, NOT <8 years old
• Teach: "No milk, no calcium, no antacids, no iron — wait 2 hours"
• Teach: "Take with full glass of water and stay upright for 30 minutes"
• Teach: "Use strong sunscreen and avoid prolonged sun exposure"
• Monitor: Oral/vaginal yeast infections, diarrhea (C. diff)
💡 Memory: "No MILK, no SUN, no BABIES, no KIDS under 8" — The 4 big tetracycline restrictions!
💊 TB Drugs — INH, Rifampin, Ethambutol
Standard TB Treatment: "RIPE"
• Rifampin
• Isoniazid (INH)
• Pyrazinamide
• Ethambutol
Duration: Intensive phase (2 months all 4 drugs) → Continuation phase (4-7 months INH + Rifampin)
🔬 Sputum Collection:
• Collect early morning specimen (best yield — organisms accumulate overnight)
• Need 3 consecutive sputum samples for AFB (acid-fast bacilli)
• Patient must cough DEEP (not just saliva) — may need sputum induction
• Negative pressure room/airborne precautions during collection
😷 Infectious Period & Airborne Precautions:
• TB spreads via airborne droplet nuclei when patient coughs/sneezes
• Airborne precautions required: N95 mask, negative pressure room
• Patient is contagious until 3 negative sputum cultures OR 2-3 weeks of effective treatment
• Non-infectious once: 3 negative AFB smears AND clinically improving AND on appropriate therapy

Isoniazid (INH)

⚠️ KEY ADVERSE EFFECTS:
• HEPATOTOXICITY — Monitor LFTs; avoid alcohol
• Peripheral neuropathy — Give pyridoxine (vitamin B6) prophylactically to prevent
• Drug interactions — Many!

Rifampin

⚠️ KEY POINTS:
• Orange/red discoloration of body fluids (urine, tears, saliva, sweat) — EXPECTED, harmless
• MASSIVE drug interactions — Induces CYP450 enzymes
• ↓Oral contraceptive effectiveness — Use backup birth control!
• Hepatotoxicity — Monitor LFTs
• Can cause flu-like syndrome

Ethambutol

⚠️ OPTIC NEURITIS (VISION CHANGES):
• Can cause decreased visual acuity and color blindness (especially red-green)
• Monitor: Baseline eye exam, monthly vision checks
• Teach: "Report any vision changes or difficulty seeing colors immediately"
• Usually reversible if caught early and drug stopped
📋 NURSING IMPLICATIONS:
• Directly Observed Therapy (DOT) — Watch patient swallow every dose (↑adherence, ↓resistance)
• Airborne precautions until non-infectious
• Contact investigation — Screen close contacts for latent TB
• Patient teaching: Complete FULL course (6-9 months), report adverse effects, no alcohol
• INH: Give vitamin B6, monitor LFTs
• Rifampin: "Your urine will turn orange — this is normal"; use backup contraception
• Ethambutol: Report vision changes immediately
💡 TB Treatment Memory: "RIPE for 2, Then IR for 4 more" — RIPE therapy for 2 months intensive, then just Isoniazid + Rifampin for 4-7 months
```

🫁 Respiratory Medications Bronchodilators & Controllers

```
🎯 Asthma Medication Overview — Controllers vs Rescue

🚨 RESCUE (Quick-Relief)

When: Acute symptoms, PRN use

Examples:

  • SABA: Albuterol (β2 agonist)
  • Anticholinergic: Ipratropium

Action: Rapid bronchodilation within minutes

🛡️ CONTROLLER (Maintenance)

When: Daily, scheduled use for prevention

Examples:

  • ICS: Inhaled corticosteroids
  • LABA: Long-acting β2 agonists
  • LTRA: Montelukast (Singulair)

Action: Reduce inflammation, prevent attacks

🎯 MDI TECHNIQUE (EXAM HIGH-YIELD):
1. Bronchodilator FIRST (e.g., albuterol) → Opens airways
2. Wait 1-2 minutes
3. Steroid SECOND (e.g., fluticasone) → Better delivery to airways
4. RINSE MOUTH after steroid → Prevents oral thrush
📋 ASSESSING BRONCHODILATOR EFFECTIVENESS:
• Before: Lung sounds (wheezing, decreased breath sounds), RR, O₂ sat, dyspnea severity, peak flow
• After (5-15 min): Reassess same parameters
• Effective: ↓wheezing, ↑breath sounds, ↑O₂ sat, ↓RR, ↓work of breathing, improved peak flow
• Not effective: Persistent/worsening symptoms → May need nebulizer, systemic steroids, or higher level of care
💊 Albuterol (SABA — Short-Acting Beta-2 Agonist)
MOA: Stimulates β2 receptors in lungs → Bronchodilation
Onset: 5-15 minutes (rapid!)
Duration: 4-6 hours
✅ USE: RESCUE inhaler for acute bronchospasm
• Asthma attack
• Exercise-induced bronchospasm (use 15-30 min before exercise)
• COPD exacerbation
⚠️ ADVERSE EFFECTS (β2 stimulation + some β1 cross-stimulation):
• Tachycardia, palpitations — β1 effects on heart
• Tremor — Very common
• Nervousness, anxiety
• HYPOKALEMIA (↓K⁺) — β2 stimulation shifts K⁺ into cells → Monitor K⁺ if frequent use!
• Hyperglycemia
⚠️ OVERUSE WARNING:
If patient needs albuterol >2 times/week for symptom relief → Asthma NOT well-controlled → Need controller medication (ICS)!
📋 NURSING:
• Assess: Lung sounds, RR, O₂ sat, HR BEFORE and AFTER
• Monitor: HR (watch for tachycardia), K⁺ levels (especially if on diuretics or digoxin)
• Teach MDI technique: Shake, exhale fully, inhale slowly while pressing, hold breath 10 sec
• Teach: "Use this as a rescue inhaler, not for daily prevention"
• Teach: "If you need this more than twice a week, call your doctor — you need a controller medication"
💡 Memory: "Albuterol = β2 = 2 lungs" — Opens airways FAST but causes tremor and ↓K⁺
💊 Ipratropium (Atrovent — Anticholinergic Bronchodilator)
MOA: Blocks muscarinic receptors in airways → Prevents bronchoconstriction → Bronchodilation
Onset: 15-30 minutes (slower than albuterol)
Duration: 4-6 hours
Use:
• COPD (first-line bronchodilator for COPD!)
• Asthma (adjunct to SABA in acute exacerbation)
• Often combined with albuterol in nebulizer (DuoNeb = albuterol + ipratropium)
⚠️ ADVERSE EFFECTS (Anticholinergic):
• Dry mouth
• Cough, throat irritation
• Headache
• Less systemic effects than albuterol (no tachycardia/tremor typically)
⚠️ EYE SAFETY WARNING:
• Avoid spraying in eyes → Can cause acute angle-closure glaucoma and mydriasis
• Use mouthpiece or mask properly to avoid eye exposure
📋 NURSING:
• Assess: Lung sounds, RR, O₂ sat before and after
• COPD patients: Often first-line; more effective than albuterol for COPD
• Teach: "Avoid spraying in your eyes", "Rinse mouth after use to prevent dryness"
• Onset slower: Takes 15-30 min to work (vs 5 min for albuterol)
💊 Inhaled Corticosteroids (ICS) — Fluticasone, Budesonide
MOA: Anti-inflammatory — Reduces airway inflammation and hyperresponsiveness
NOT a bronchodilator! Does not provide immediate relief
✅ USE: CONTROLLER medication for asthma
• First-line maintenance therapy for persistent asthma
• Prevents asthma attacks
• Taken DAILY, even when feeling well
⚠️ ADVERSE EFFECTS:
• Oral thrush (candidiasis) — #1 local side effect
• Hoarseness, sore throat
• Cough
• Minimal systemic effects at therapeutic doses (unlike oral steroids)
🚨 THRUSH PREVENTION:
RINSE MOUTH AND GARGLE after every use → Spit out (don't swallow)
Use spacer device → ↓oral deposition → ↓thrush risk
📋 NURSING:
• NOT for acute relief! Takes days to weeks for full effect
• Teach: "Use this every day, even when you feel good — it prevents attacks, doesn't stop them"
• Teach: "Rinse your mouth thoroughly after each use to prevent yeast infection"
• Teach: "Use your albuterol (rescue inhaler) FIRST, then wait, then use this steroid inhaler"
• Monitor: Oral cavity for thrush (white patches), hoarseness
• Growth monitoring in children: High doses may slow growth slightly
💡 Memory: "ICS = Inside Control Steroid" — Use daily for CONTROL, rinse mouth after to prevent thrush
💊 LTRA — Montelukast (Singulair)
MOA: Leukotriene receptor antagonist — Blocks inflammatory mediators
Route: ORAL (tablet or chewable)
NOT an inhaler!
✅ USE: Controller medication
• Asthma maintenance (alternative/add-on to ICS)
• Allergic rhinitis
• Exercise-induced bronchospasm prevention
• Taken ONCE DAILY, usually at bedtime
🚨 FDA BLACK BOX WARNING (2020):
Neuropsychiatric events: Agitation, aggression, depression, suicidal thoughts/behavior, sleep disturbances
Monitor closely for mood/behavior changes!
Consider alternatives if patient has history of psychiatric illness
⚠️ OTHER ADVERSE EFFECTS:
• Headache
• GI upset
• Upper respiratory infection
• Generally well-tolerated
📋 NURSING:
• NOT for acute rescue! Takes days for full effect
• Assess mental health: Screen for depression, anxiety, mood changes at every visit
• Teach: "Take this every day for prevention, not during an attack"
• Teach: "Report any mood changes, depression, or unusual behavior immediately"
• Teach: "This is a pill, not an inhaler — take with or without food"
```

💊 Pain Management Opioids, NSAIDs, Tylenol

```
🧠 Types of Pain
TypeDescriptionExamplesTreatment
Acute Pain Sudden onset, specific cause, limited duration (<3-6 months) Post-surgical pain, trauma, childbirth Opioids, NSAIDs, acetaminophen
Chronic Pain Persists >3-6 months beyond expected healing Arthritis, fibromyalgia, chronic back pain Multimodal: NSAIDs, adjuvants, non-pharm
Nociceptive Tissue damage detected by nociceptors Cut, burn, bone fracture, surgical incision Opioids, NSAIDs, local anesthetics
Neuropathic Nerve damage; burning, shooting, tingling Diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia Gabapentin, TCAs, SNRIs; opioids less effective
Breakthrough Pain Transient flare of severe pain despite around-the-clock analgesia Cancer pain spike with movement IV opioid (fast-acting) PRN
🎯 BREAKTHROUGH PAIN — EXAM FOCUS:
• Definition: Sudden, severe pain that "breaks through" baseline controlled pain
• Treatment: RAPID-ONSET opioid (IV preferred for fastest relief)
• Do NOT: Wait for next scheduled dose or give long-acting opioid
• Reassess: 15-30 min after IV dose; may need additional rescue doses
💊 NSAIDs — Ibuprofen, Naproxen, Ketorolac
MOA: Inhibit COX enzymes → ↓prostaglandin synthesis → ↓inflammation & pain
Uses: Pain + Fever + Inflammation
• Mild to moderate pain
• Inflammatory conditions (arthritis, gout)
• Fever
• Dysmenorrhea
🚫 CONTRAINDICATIONS (HIGH-YIELD):
1. Active bleeding or bleeding disorders
2. Anticoagulant therapy (warfarin, heparin, DOACs) — ↑bleeding risk!
3. Renal disease — NSAIDs ↓renal blood flow → acute kidney injury
4. Peptic ulcer disease or GI bleeding history — NSAIDs ↑GI bleed risk
5. Severe heart failure (fluid retention)
6. Aspirin allergy (cross-sensitivity)
⚠️ ADVERSE EFFECTS:
• GI bleeding/ulceration — #1 serious adverse effect; take with food
• Acute kidney injury — Especially in elderly, dehydrated, CKD patients
• ↑bleeding risk — Inhibits platelet function
• Cardiovascular events (MI, stroke) with long-term use
• Fluid retention, edema, HTN
📋 NURSING:
• Before giving: Check for contraindications (bleeding disorder, anticoagulants, renal disease)
• Monitor: BUN/Cr (kidney function), stool for occult blood, BP
• Teach: "Take with food or milk to protect your stomach"
• Teach: "Report black/tarry stools, coffee-ground vomitus, or unusual bruising" (GI bleed signs)
• Teach: "Do NOT combine with aspirin or other NSAIDs" (↑toxicity)
• Elderly: Higher risk for GI bleeding and kidney injury — use lowest effective dose
💡 Memory: "NSAIDs = No Stomach, Anticoags, Impaired kidneys, Dialysis" — The contraindications!
💊 Acetaminophen (Tylenol)
MOA: Inhibits COX in CNS → ↓pain & fever
KEY: NO anti-inflammatory effect (doesn't work on COX in periphery)
Uses: Pain + Fever (NOT inflammation)
• Mild to moderate pain
• Fever reduction
• Safer than NSAIDs for patients with bleeding risk, renal disease, or GI issues
🚨 HEPATOTOXICITY — #1 CONCERN:
Maximum dose: 4000 mg/day (4 grams)
Elderly/liver disease: 3000 mg/day max
Overdose causes:
• Acute liver failure (can be fatal!)
• Symptoms may be delayed 24-48 hours
• Antidote: N-acetylcysteine (NAC) — most effective if given within 8 hours
⚠️ HIDDEN ACETAMINOPHEN DANGER:
Many OTC products contain acetaminophen:
• Combination cold/flu medications
• Percocet (oxycodone + acetaminophen)
• Vicodin (hydrocodone + acetaminophen)
→ Easy to accidentally exceed max dose!
📋 NURSING:
• Before giving: Ask about liver disease, alcohol use (↑hepatotoxicity risk)
• Calculate total daily dose from ALL sources (OTC, prescription combos)
• Monitor: LFTs (AST, ALT) if long-term use or risk factors
• Teach: "Do not exceed 4000 mg (4 grams) per day — this includes all products with acetaminophen"
• Teach: "Read labels! Many cold medicines contain acetaminophen"
• Teach: "Avoid alcohol while taking this — increases liver damage risk"
• Overdose: Treat immediately with NAC; most effective within 8 hours
💡 Memory: "Tylenol = 4000 mg Top, or Toxicity Takes over" — Don't exceed 4g/day!
🚫 Aspirin in Children — Reye Syndrome
⚠️ CONTRAINDICATION: Aspirin in children/teens with viral illness
Risk: Reye Syndrome — Rare but potentially fatal
Pathophysiology: Acute encephalopathy + fatty liver degeneration
Symptoms: Vomiting, lethargy, confusion, seizures, coma
Mortality: 20-40% if untreated
🚫 DO NOT give aspirin to:
• Children <18 years with fever or viral illness (especially flu, chickenpox)
• Use acetaminophen or ibuprofen instead
📋 NURSING:
• Teach parents: "Never give aspirin to children with flu or chickenpox"
• Check labels: Some OTC products contain aspirin (aspirin = salicylate = ASA)
• Exception: Aspirin may be prescribed for children with Kawasaki disease (antiplatelet effect) — but this is MD decision
💊 Morphine & Opioids
MOA: Bind to mu (μ) opioid receptors in CNS → ↓pain perception
Common Opioids:
• Morphine — Prototype; IV/PO/epidural
• Hydromorphone (Dilaudid) — 5-7x more potent than morphine
• Fentanyl — 50-100x more potent; IV/transdermal patch
• Oxycodone — PO; often combined with acetaminophen (Percocet)
• Hydrocodone — PO; combined with acetaminophen (Vicodin)
🚨 OPIOID OVERDOSE/TOXICITY — PRIORITY NURSING:
TRIAD:
1. RESPIRATORY DEPRESSION — RR <12, shallow, irregular
2. CNS DEPRESSION — Sedation, stupor, coma
3. MIOSIS — Pinpoint pupils (except meperidine)
⚠️ #1 PRIORITY: RESPIRATORY ASSESSMENT
Before EVERY opioid dose:
• Assess: RR, depth, rhythm, O₂ saturation, level of consciousness
• Hold opioid if: RR <12 (or <8-10 depending on policy), O₂ sat <90%, excessive sedation
• After giving opioid: Reassess q15-30min initially, then q1-2hr
⚠️ OTHER ADVERSE EFFECTS:
• Constipation — Occurs in almost ALL patients; give stool softener/stimulant prophylactically
• Nausea/vomiting — Common initially, often improves
• Pruritus (itching) — Histamine release, not true allergy
• Urinary retention
• Sedation, confusion (especially elderly)
• Hypotension (vasodilation)
📋 NURSING IMPLICATIONS:
• Monitor: RR, sedation level, pain score, O₂ sat, BP (PRIORITY: respiratory status)
• Hold parameters: RR <12, sedation score ≥3, O₂ sat <90%
• IV push: Give SLOWLY (over 4-5 min) to prevent respiratory depression
• Constipation prevention: Give stool softener (docusate) + stimulant (senna) prophylactically
• Safety: Fall precautions (sedation, orthostatic hypotension), bed alarm, call bell within reach
• Do NOT combine with other CNS depressants (benzos, alcohol) → Synergistic respiratory depression!
💊 Naloxone (Narcan) — Opioid Antidote
MOA: Opioid antagonist — Competitively blocks mu receptors → Reverses opioid effects
🚨 INDICATIONS:
• Opioid overdose — Respiratory depression, CNS depression, miosis
• Respiratory depression from therapeutic opioid use
• Suspected opioid overdose (street drugs)
⚠️ SHORT-ACTING!
Duration: 30-90 minutes
Many opioids last LONGER than naloxone
→ Respiratory depression can RETURN after naloxone wears off!
→ Must monitor closely for several hours
→ May need repeat doses or continuous infusion
⚠️ ADVERSE EFFECTS OF RAPID REVERSAL:
• Acute withdrawal syndrome — Agitation, tachycardia, HTN, N/V, pain
• Severe pain — Reverses ALL opioid analgesia
• Pulmonary edema (rare but serious)
• Seizures (rare)
📋 NURSING — Opioid Overdose:
1. Assess ABCs — Airway patent? Breathing adequate? Pulse present?
2. Stimulate patient — "Can you hear me?" Sternal rub if needed
3. Give naloxone:
• IV: 0.4-2 mg IV push (can repeat q2-3min; max 10 mg)
• IM/SubQ/Intranasal: Available for community/field use
4. Support respirations — Bag-valve-mask if needed, O₂
5. Monitor continuously — VS, RR, sedation level, O₂ sat
6. Watch for re-sedation — Naloxone wears off before opioid! May need repeat doses
7. Pain management — Patient may have severe pain after reversal; consider non-opioid options initially
💡 Memory: "Narcan is Short, So See Sedation Soon" — Short duration (30-90 min), watch for return of respiratory depression!
```

```
💊 Parenteral vs Enteral Routes

Enteral (GI Tract)

Routes: Oral (PO), NG/PEG tube, Rectal

Characteristics:

  • Slower onset
  • First-pass metabolism (oral)
  • Variable absorption
  • Patient must have functioning GI tract
  • Lower bioavailability than IV

Parenteral (Bypass GI)

Routes: IV, IM, SubQ, Intradermal, Intrathecal

Characteristics:

  • Faster onset (IV = immediate)
  • NO first-pass metabolism
  • More predictable absorption
  • Higher bioavailability
  • Useful if NPO, vomiting, unconscious
🎯 CLINICAL DECISION:
Choose Parenteral when:
• Rapid effect needed (emergency)
• NPO, vomiting, altered LOC
• Drug destroyed by stomach acid
• GI dysfunction/malabsorption
Choose Enteral when:
• Patient can swallow and tolerate PO
• Non-urgent situation
• Long-term maintenance therapy
• Cost/convenience considerations
🌿 Herbal Treatments & Anticoagulant Interactions
⚠️ CRITICAL CONCEPT:
"Natural" does NOT mean safe! Many herbals interact with prescription medications, especially anticoagulants.
Herbal/SupplementEffect with AnticoagulantsMechanism
Ginkgo Biloba ↑Bleeding risk Inhibits platelet aggregation; potentiates warfarin
Garlic ↑Bleeding risk Antiplatelet effects; may ↑INR on warfarin
Ginger ↑Bleeding risk Inhibits thromboxane synthetase (antiplatelet)
Ginseng ↓Warfarin effect ↓INR (mechanism unclear); also ↑bleeding with antiplatelet agents
St. John's Wort ↓Warfarin effect CYP450 inducer → ↑warfarin metabolism → ↓INR
Vitamin E (high dose) ↑Bleeding risk Inhibits platelet aggregation; potentiates anticoagulants
Fish Oil/Omega-3 ↑Bleeding risk Antiplatelet effects; ↓thromboxane A2
📋 NURSING IMPLICATIONS:
• ALWAYS ask about herbal/supplement use during medication reconciliation
• Patients on anticoagulants: Teach to avoid herbals with bleeding risk; consult MD before starting ANY supplement
• Warfarin patients: Check INR more frequently if herbal use suspected
• Teach: "Natural does not mean safe — herbals can interact with your medications"
• Teach: "Tell ALL your healthcare providers about every supplement you take"
💡 Memory: "The 3 G's ↑Bleed: Ginkgo, Garlic, Ginger" — All increase bleeding with anticoagulants!
👴 Age-Related Changes in Pharmacokinetics
WHY IT MATTERS: Elderly patients at ↑risk for adverse drug reactions and toxicity due to age-related changes in ADME

Absorption (Usually minimal change)

  • ↓Gastric acid → May ↓absorption of some drugs
  • ↓GI motility → Slower absorption
  • Clinical impact: Minimal for most drugs

Distribution (Significant changes)

  • ↓Total body water → ↑concentration of water-soluble drugs (e.g., digoxin)
  • ↑Body fat → ↑accumulation of fat-soluble drugs (e.g., benzodiazepines, anesthetics)
  • ↓Serum albumin → ↑FREE (active) drug levels of protein-bound drugs (e.g., warfarin, phenytoin)
  • Clinical impact: Standard doses may cause toxicity

Metabolism (Significant changes)

  • ↓Liver mass and hepatic blood flow
  • ↓CYP450 enzyme activity
  • → ↓drug metabolism → ↑half-life → accumulation
  • Clinical impact: CNS depressants (opioids, benzos) especially dangerous

Excretion (MOST SIGNIFICANT)

  • ↓GFR (glomerular filtration rate) — Progressive decline with age
  • ↓Renal blood flow
  • ↓Tubular secretion
  • → ↓drug clearance → accumulation → TOXICITY
  • Note: Serum creatinine may appear "normal" due to ↓muscle mass, but eGFR/CrCl shows renal impairment
  • Clinical impact: Dose adjustments needed for renally cleared drugs (aminoglycosides, vancomycin, metformin, digoxin)
🎯 EXAM PRINCIPLE: "Start low, go slow" in elderly
→ Use lowest effective dose and titrate slowly
📋 NURSING:
• Calculate eGFR/CrCl — Don't rely on serum creatinine alone in elderly
• Monitor closely for adverse effects, especially CNS depression and orthostatic hypotension
• Drug safety: Beers Criteria = list of potentially inappropriate meds for elderly (anticholinergics, benzos, long-acting opioids)
• Polypharmacy: Elderly often on multiple meds → ↑drug interaction risk
• Teach: Keep updated med list, bring ALL meds (including OTC/herbals) to appointments
🩺 Nursing Process (ADPIE)
🎯 MEMORIZE THE ORDER: Assessment → Diagnosis → Planning → Implementation → Evaluation
StepWhat You DoExample
1. Assessment Collect data (subjective + objective) VS, pain scale, breath sounds, medication history, allergies
2. Diagnosis Identify nursing problems "Ineffective airway clearance r/t excessive secretions"
3. Planning Set goals & interventions Goal: "Patient will maintain O2 sat >92%"
Intervention: "Administer albuterol q4h"
4. Implementation Carry out the plan Give albuterol, monitor response, document
5. Evaluation Did it work? Reassess. Check O2 sat after treatment, adjust plan if needed
📌 EXAM TIP: When prioritizing nursing actions, Assessment ALWAYS comes first unless the patient is in immediate danger (then you implement life-saving interventions first, THEN assess).
🦠 TB Infective Period & Isolation
⚠️ HIGH-YIELD: Know when TB patients are contagious and when isolation can be discontinued.

When is TB Contagious?

  • BEFORE treatment starts — Patient is most contagious
  • First 2-3 weeks of treatment — Still potentially infectious
  • After 2-3 weeks on effective therapy — Usually no longer contagious if:
    • Clinical improvement (↓cough, ↓fever)
    • 3 consecutive negative sputum cultures
    • Adherence to medication regimen confirmed
🎯 ISOLATION REQUIREMENTS:
Type: Airborne precautions
Room: Negative pressure room
PPE: N95 respirator (not just a surgical mask!)
Duration: Until 3 consecutive negative sputum cultures AND clinical improvement
💡 MEMORY TIP: TB = Three negatives to Be free (3 negative sputum cultures)

🩸 Hemoglobin (Hgb) Monitoring with Anticoagulants
🎯 WHY MONITOR Hgb/Hct?
Anticoagulants ↑bleeding risk → Blood loss → ↓Hgb/Hct → Anemia

Normal Values

Hemoglobin: Males 14-18 g/dL, Females 12-16 g/dL

Hematocrit: Males 42-52%, Females 37-47%

💉 LMWH (Low Molecular Weight Heparin)
🎯 KEY DIFFERENCE: LMWH vs Unfractionated Heparin
LMWH: NO aPTT monitoring needed
UFH: aPTT required
🩸 Thrombolytics — What Are They For?
⚠️ CRITICAL CONCEPT: Thrombolytics DISSOLVE existing clots
(Anticoagulants just PREVENT new clots from forming)
🎯 INDICATIONS:
1. Acute MI (STEMI) - <12 hours
2. Ischemic Stroke - <4.5 hours (critical!)
3. Massive PE
4. Extensive DVT
💡 MEMORY TIP: TIME = TISSUE → Must give within narrow time window!
```
