The Process: Patient Infected → What To Do BEFORE Giving Antibiotics

CULTURE & SENSITIVITY BEFORE FIRST DOSE — This WILL be on the exam!
⚠️ THE GOLDEN RULE: Culture FIRST, then Antibiotics!
1
Recognize Signs of Infection: Fever, ↑WBC, redness, swelling, warmth, pain, purulent drainage, ↑HR, ↑RR, altered mental status. Labs: ↑WBC with left shift, ↑CRP, ↑ESR, positive blood cultures, ↑procalcitonin.
↓
2
COLLECT CULTURE SPECIMENS BEFORE FIRST ANTIBIOTIC DOSE! Blood cultures (2 sets from 2 different sites), urine C&S, wound culture, sputum culture — whatever is appropriate for the suspected infection site. WHY: Antibiotics kill bacteria → if you give the drug first, the culture may come back falsely negative → you won't know what organism you're fighting or what drugs will kill it.
↓
3
Start Empiric Therapy: Begin broad-spectrum antibiotics IMMEDIATELY after cultures are drawn. Don't wait for results (takes 24-72 hours). The patient needs treatment NOW. This is empiric therapy — best guess based on likely organisms.
↓
4
Culture Results Return (24-72 hrs): Lab identifies the exact organism AND which drugs it's sensitive/resistant to (sensitivity report).
↓
5
De-escalation / Definitive Therapy: Narrow the antibiotic from broad-spectrum to targeted therapy based on C&S results. Using the narrowest effective drug reduces superinfection risk, cost, and resistance development.
🧠 "Culture Before Cocktail"
"SWAB then STAB" — Get the specimen THEN start the IV antibiotic!
Never give the antibiotic before collecting cultures. Once antibiotics are in the system, they start killing bacteria → culture may come back negative even though the patient IS infected. You lose your chance to identify the bug and its weaknesses.
🏥 REAL-LIFE SCENARIO — The ER Sepsis Patient

A 72-year-old arrives in the ED with fever 103°F, HR 110, BP 88/52, confused, WBC 18,000 with left shift. Suspected sepsis from UTI.

What does the nurse do?

  • FIRST: Draw blood cultures × 2 sets from different sites + urine C&S + lactic acid level
  • THEN: Start broad-spectrum IV antibiotics within 1 hour of recognition (Sepsis Hour-1 Bundle)
  • THEN: IV fluids (30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension)
  • LATER: When C&S results return → narrow antibiotics to targeted therapy
  • THE MISTAKE: Starting antibiotics BEFORE drawing cultures → you'll never know the exact bug

Key Teachings for ALL Patients on Antibiotics

These Patient Education Points Come Up on EVERY Antibiotic Exam!
🩺 Universal Antibiotic Patient Education
  • Complete the ENTIRE course — even if you feel better. Stopping early → resistant bacteria survive → relapse with harder-to-treat infection
  • Take at the correct time — some need empty stomach (penicillin), some need food (amoxicillin-clavulanate). Follow directions exactly.
  • Report signs of allergic reaction IMMEDIATELY: Rash, hives, swelling (lips/tongue/throat), difficulty breathing, wheezing → ANAPHYLAXIS = call 911
  • Watch for superinfection: New diarrhea (especially watery/bloody → C. diff), white patches in mouth (thrush), vaginal itching/discharge (yeast). Report immediately.
  • Take probiotics or yogurt with antibiotics to help maintain normal flora (separate from antibiotic dose by 2 hours)
  • No alcohol with metronidazole or cephalosporins (disulfiram reaction: N/V, flushing, tachycardia)
  • Oral contraceptives may be less effective — use backup birth control (especially with rifampin)
  • Don't share antibiotics — each infection needs specific treatment
  • Don't save leftover antibiotics — there shouldn't be leftovers if you complete the course

Microbial Infection Basics

Microorganisms & Body Defenses

Microorganisms are everywhere — can be harmful or beneficial under normal circumstances.

Body's defenses against infection:

  • Physical barriers: Intact skin, ciliated respiratory mucosa
  • Physiologic defenses: Stomach gastric acid, antibodies
  • Phagocytic cells: White blood cells that engulf and destroy pathogens
Gram-Negative vs. Gram-Positive Bacteria
Both types of bacteria cause infections, but gram-negative bacteria are generally WORSE (more difficult to treat, more drug-resistant, cause more serious infections). This is because gram-negative bacteria have an outer membrane that provides an extra layer of protection against many antibiotics.

Colonization, Infection & Disease

Know the Differences!
  • Colonization: Microorganisms are PRESENT on or in the body but are NOT causing disease or symptoms. The person is not sick. Example: MRSA can colonize the skin or nares without causing infection.
  • Infection: Microorganisms have INVADED the body and are multiplying in tissues, triggering an immune response. Signs/symptoms may or may not be present yet.
  • Disease: The infection is causing clear signs and symptoms — the patient is clinically ill (fever, inflammation, pain, etc.).
💡 Key Distinction: A person can be colonized with an organism (like MRSA on their skin) without having an active infection. Colonization does NOT require antibiotic treatment. Infection and disease DO.

Infection Sites of Origin

Community-Associated Infections

An infection acquired by a person who has NOT been hospitalized or had a medical procedure (dialysis, surgery, catheterization) within the past year.

Health Care–Associated Infections (HAIs)
HIGH YIELD
  • Contracted in a hospital or institutional setting
  • Were NOT present or incubating on admission
  • Occurs more than 48 hours after admission
  • More difficult to treat because organisms are often drug-resistant and the most virulent
  • MRSA (Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) is the MOST COMMON health care–associated infection
  • Previously called nosocomial infections

Antiseptics vs. Disinfectants

🧴 Antiseptics

What they do: Generally only INHIBIT the growth of microorganisms (do not necessarily kill them)

Used on: LIVING tissue only (skin, wounds)

Classification: Static agents (bacteriostatic)

Example: Hand sanitizer, Betadine on skin

🧪 Disinfectants

What they do: KILL organisms

Used on: NON-LIVING objects only (surfaces, equipment, instruments)

Classification: Cidal agents (bactericidal)

Example: Bleach on countertops, instrument sterilization

Prevention of HAIs starts with HANDWASHING — the single most important prevention measure.

Why We Use Antibiotics

Antibiotics = Medications Used to Treat BACTERIAL Infections

They do NOT work against viruses, fungi, or parasites (different drug classes for those).

⚠️ ALWAYS GET A CULTURE FIRST!
Ideally, before beginning antibiotic therapy, the suspected areas of infection should be cultured to identify the causative organism and potential antibiotic susceptibilities. This ensures the RIGHT antibiotic is used and helps prevent resistance.

Types of Antibiotic Therapy

Know ALL of these definitions — they are testable:
  • Empiric therapy: Treatment of an infection BEFORE specific culture information has been reported or obtained. This is a "best guess" based on suspected organism and common patterns. Started immediately, then adjusted later when culture results come back.
  • Definitive therapy: Antibiotic therapy tailored to treat the specific organism identified with cultures. The right drug for the right bug.
  • Prophylactic therapy: Treatment with antibiotics to PREVENT an infection, such as before intraabdominal surgery or after trauma. Given BEFORE infection occurs.

Therapeutic vs. Subtherapeutic Response

Therapeutic Response ✅

The antibiotic is WORKING. You see a decrease in specific signs and symptoms of infection:

  • Fever is coming down
  • Elevated WBC count is normalizing
  • Redness, inflammation decreasing
  • Drainage decreasing
  • Pain improving
  • Patient reports feeling better
Subtherapeutic Response ❌

Signs and symptoms of infection do NOT improve. The antibiotic is not effective. This could mean:

  • Wrong antibiotic for the organism
  • Drug-resistant organism
  • Inadequate dose or duration
  • Patient nonadherence
  • Need to reassess and possibly change therapy

Superinfection vs. Secondary Infection

Know the DIFFERENCE and be able to give EXAMPLES:

Superinfection

A NEW infection that develops DURING antibiotic treatment because the antibiotic killed off normal flora, allowing opportunistic organisms to overgrow.

Classic examples:

• C. difficile (formerly pseudomembranous colitis) — overgrowth in GI tract after normal flora killed

• Candida (yeast) infections — oral thrush, vaginal candidiasis

Secondary Infection

A NEW, DIFFERENT infection that develops as a complication of the original infection or from a weakened immune state — NOT directly caused by antibiotic use.

Example:

• Patient has flu (viral) → develops bacterial pneumonia as a secondary infection

• Wound infection → leads to sepsis

Key difference: Superinfection is caused BY the antibiotic (killing normal flora). Secondary infection is caused by the PRIMARY disease process weakening the body.

Signs of Superinfection to Monitor

Fever, perineal itching, cough, lethargy, or any unusual discharge

Allergic Reactions

Antibiotic Allergic Reactions
TESTABLE
  • Penicillins and sulfonamides are the two broad classes of antibiotics to which many people have allergic anaphylactic reactions
  • Most common SEVERE reactions:
    • Difficulty breathing
    • Significant rash, hives, or other skin reaction
    • Severe GI intolerance
  • Pregnancy-related host factors — some antibiotics are not safe in pregnancy
  • Genetic host factors: G6PD deficiency, slow acetylation
  • Anatomic site of infection matters for drug selection (some drugs don't penetrate certain tissues)
💡 Nursing Pearl: When a patient says they're "allergic" to an antibiotic, ALWAYS ask what kind of reaction they had. Some patients report "allergies" that were actually common adverse effects (like GI upset). True allergy = hives, angioedema, throat swelling, anaphylaxis. Clarify with open-ended questions BEFORE documenting.

Mechanism of Action

4 Ways Antibiotics Work (Know ALL Four):
  1. Interference with cell wall synthesis (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, vancomycin)
  2. Interference with protein synthesis (macrolides, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides)
  3. Interference with DNA replication (quinolones/fluoroquinolones)
  4. Acting as antimetabolites to disrupt critical metabolic reactions inside the bacterial cell (sulfonamides)

Sites of activity: Cell wall synthesis, DNA replication, RNA replication, antimetabolites, protein synthesis at 30S ribosomes, protein synthesis at 50S ribosomes

Resistance

Bacteria can develop resistance to antibiotics — this is a growing concern. Causes include: overuse of antibiotics, not completing full course of therapy, use in agriculture. Resistance is why we have MRSA, VRE, and other MDROs.

Sulfonamides

Overview
  • One of the FIRST groups of antibiotics ever developed
  • Often combined with another antibiotic for better effectiveness
  • Most important combination: Sulfamethoxazole + Trimethoprim (SMX-TMP)
  • Trade names: Bactrim, Septra, or co-trimoxazole
  • Trimethoprim is a NON-sulfonamide antibiotic combined with the sulfa drug
  • Commonly used in clinical practice
Sulfonamide MOA (Mechanism of Action):
  • Bacteriostatic action (inhibits, doesn't kill directly)
  • Prevents synthesis of folic acid — which bacteria need to make purines and nucleic acids
  • Does NOT affect human cells because humans can use preformed folic acid from diet
  • Only affects organisms that synthesize their own folic acid

Indications

Sulfonamide Indications
BROAD SPECTRUM
  • Effective against BOTH gram-positive AND gram-negative bacteria
  • Primary use: UTIs caused by susceptible strains:
    • Enterobacter spp.
    • Escherichia coli
    • Klebsiella spp.
    • Proteus mirabilis
    • Proteus vulgaris
    • Staphylococcus aureus
Commonly associated with HIV:
Co-trimoxazole (SMX-TMP / Bactrim) is used to treat Pneumocystis jiroveci pneumonia — an opportunistic infection commonly seen in HIV/AIDS patients. This is a HIGH-YIELD connection on exams.
Other Sulfonamide Uses
  • Upper respiratory tract infections
  • SMX-TMP for outpatient Staph infections — because of the high rate of community-acquired MRSA

Penicillins

Penicillin Subgroups

Penicillin MOA:
  1. Penicillins enter the bacteria via the cell wall
  2. Inside the cell, they bind to penicillin-binding protein
  3. Once bound, normal cell wall synthesis is disrupted
  4. Result: Bacteria cells die from cell lysis

Penicillins do NOT kill other cells in the body — only bacteria with cell walls. This is why they are BACTERICIDAL.

Indications

Penicillin Indications
TESTABLE

Prevention and treatment of infections caused by susceptible bacteria, primarily:

  • Gram-POSITIVE bacteria (this is their main target)
  • Including: Streptococcus spp., Enterococcus spp., Staphylococcus spp.

Beta-Lactamase Inhibitors & Combination Agents

Beta-Lactamase Inhibitors: Some bacteria produce an enzyme called beta-lactamase that breaks down the penicillin molecule, making it useless. Beta-lactamase inhibitors bind with that enzyme to PREVENT breakdown of the penicillin, allowing it to work.

The 3 inhibitors:

  • Clavulanic acid (clavulanate)
  • Tazobactam
  • Sulbactam

Adverse Effects

Penicillin Adverse Effects
  • Allergic reactions occur in 0.7% to 4% of treatment courses
  • Urticaria (hives), pruritus (itching), angioedema (swelling)
  • Patients allergic to penicillins have an increased risk of allergy to other beta-lactam antibiotics (cross-sensitivity with cephalosporins)
  • Ask what KIND of reaction occurred — this is essential
  • Patients with history of throat swelling or hives from penicillin → should NOT receive cephalosporins
  • Common (non-allergic) adverse effects: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain
Pregnancy Category B = Generally SAFE
Penicillins are usually safe and well-tolerated medications. They are Pregnancy Category B, meaning animal studies have shown no risk and they are generally considered safe in pregnancy.

Cephalosporins

Overview
  • Semisynthetic antibiotics
  • Structurally and pharmacologically RELATED TO PENICILLINS
  • Bactericidal action
  • Broad spectrum
  • Divided into 5 generations based on antimicrobial activity
  • As you go UP in generations: gram-negative coverage gets BETTER, gram-positive coverage decreases (until 4th/5th gen which broaden back out)

First Generation

1st Generation Cephalosporins — Focus on Cefazolin & Cephalexin:
  • Good gram-POSITIVE coverage, poor gram-negative coverage
  • Parenteral and oral (PO) forms available
  • Used for surgical prophylaxis and susceptible staphylococcal infections
  • Cefazolin (Ancef, Kefzol): IV or IM — the go-to for surgical prophylaxis
  • Cephalexin (Keflex): PO form — common oral first-gen cephalosporin
  • Other examples: Cefadroxil (Duricef), Cephradine (Velosef)

Second Generation

2nd Generation Cephalosporins
  • Good gram-positive coverage + BETTER gram-negative than 1st gen
  • Cefoxitin (Mefoxin): IV and IM; used prophylactically for abdominal or colorectal surgeries; also kills anaerobes
  • Cefuroxime: Zinacef (IV/IM) and Ceftin (PO); surgical prophylaxis; does NOT kill anaerobes
  • Others: Cefaclor (Ceclor), Cefprozil (Cefzil), Cefotetan (Cefotan)

Third Generation

3rd Gen = Most potent against GRAM-NEGATIVE, less active against gram-positive

⭐ Ceftriaxone (Rocephin) — KNOW THIS DRUG:

  • IV and IM
  • Long half-life → once-a-day dosing
  • Elimination is primarily HEPATIC (liver, not kidneys)
  • Easily passes meninges → diffuses into CSF → treats CNS infections (meningitis)

Ceftazidime (Fortaz):

  • IV and IM
  • Excellent gram-negative coverage
  • Used for difficult-to-treat organisms like Pseudomonas spp.
  • Resistance is limiting usefulness

Fourth & Fifth Generations

4th Generation

The Dairy/Chelation Rule

⚠️ #1 TESTABLE POINT — DAIRY PRODUCTS & TETRACYCLINES:

Tetracyclines bind (chelate) to Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, and Al³⁺ ions to form insoluble complexes.

DO NOT take with:
• Dairy products (milk, cheese, yogurt) — calcium
• Antacids — calcium, magnesium, aluminum
• Iron salts/supplements
• Enteral feedings

All of these REDUCE oral absorption of tetracyclines, making them less effective.
⚠️ #2 TESTABLE POINT — AGE & PREGNANCY RESTRICTIONS:

Should NOT be used in:
• Children younger than age 8 years
• Pregnant women
• Lactating (breastfeeding) women

WHY? The drug binds to calcium in developing teeth causing permanent discoloration (mottling) of teeth and tooth enamel in fetuses, children, and nursing infants if taken by the mother.

May also retard fetal skeletal development if taken during pregnancy.

General Antibiotic Nursing Implications

🩺 Before Beginning Therapy (ALL Antibiotics)
  • Assess drug allergies — especially penicillin and sulfa
  • Assess renal, liver, and cardiac function and other lab studies
  • Obtain thorough patient health history including immune status
  • Assess for contraindications and conditions requiring cautious use
  • Assess for potential drug interactions
⚠️ ALWAYS:
• Obtain cultures from appropriate sites BEFORE beginning antibiotic therapy
• Instruct patients to take antibiotics exactly as prescribed for the FULL length of time — do NOT stop early because they feel better
• All oral antibiotics are absorbed better with at least 6-8 oz of WATER
🩺 Ongoing Monitoring (ALL Antibiotics)
  • Assess for signs/symptoms of superinfection: fever, perineal itching, cough, lethargy, any unusual discharge
  • Check medication names carefully — many drugs sound alike or have similar spellings
  • Most common AEs of antibiotics overall: nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea
🩺 Monitor for Therapeutic Effects
  • Improvement of signs/symptoms of infection
  • Return to normal vital signs
  • Negative culture and sensitivity tests
  • Disappearance of fever, lethargy, drainage, and redness
  • Also monitor for adverse reactions specific to each drug class

Multidrug-Resistant Organisms (MDROs)

Definition: Organisms that are resistant to one or more classes of antimicrobial drugs. Know ALL FOUR names and what makes each one unique:
1. MRSA — Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus
MOST COMMON HAI
  • Threat of becoming resistant to ALL antibiotics currently available
  • No longer just a hospital problem — has spread to the COMMUNITY
  • Approximately 50% of staphylococcal infections contracted in the community involve MRSA
  • This is a community-based bacteria now — not just healthcare-associated
  • Treatment: Vancomycin (drug of choice), linezolid, daptomycin, ceftaroline (5th gen cephalosporin)
2. VRE — Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococcus
TESTABLE
  • Usually seen in urinary tract infections (UTIs)
  • Resistant to vancomycin — which used to be the "drug of last resort"
  • Newer antibiotics developed to treat VRE: linezolid (Zyvox), quinupristin-dalfopristin (Synercid), daptomycin (Cubicin)
3. ESBL — Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamase Producers
TESTABLE
  • Organisms that produce ESBL are resistant to ALL beta-lactam antibiotics AND aztreonam
  • Can be treated ONLY with carbapenems or sometimes quinolones
  • Problem: Use of carbapenems led to resistance → organisms produce carbapenemase, which renders ALL carbapenems ineffective
  • When carbapenems fail → tigecycline and colistimethate are last-resort options
4. CRO — Carbapenem-Resistant Organisms

(Formerly called CRE — Carbapenem-Resistant Enterobacteriaceae)

  • Resistant to carbapenems (the broadest-spectrum antibiotics)
  • Extremely difficult to treat
  • Very limited treatment options: tigecycline, colistimethate
⚠️ Resistance Chain to Remember:
Regular bacteria → resistant to penicillins → MRSA
MRSA treated with vancomycin → resistant to vancomycin → VRE
Beta-lactam resistant → ESBL → treated with carbapenems
Carbapenem resistant → CRO → last resort: tigecycline, colistimethate

This is why we MUST use antibiotics responsibly and patients MUST complete full courses!

Mechanism of Action

Aminoglycoside MOA:
  • BACTERICIDAL — they KILL bacteria (not just inhibit growth)
  • Prevent protein synthesis (bind to 30S ribosomal subunit)
  • Kill mostly GRAM-NEGATIVE bacteria: Pseudomonas spp., E. coli, Proteus spp., Klebsiella spp., Serratia spp.
  • Also some gram-positive bacteria

Indications

Aminoglycoside Indications
TESTABLE
  • Gram-negative infections: Pseudomonas, E. coli, Proteus, Klebsiella, Serratia
  • Often used in COMBINATION with other antibiotics for synergistic effects:
    • With beta-lactams (penicillins/cephalosporins)
    • With vancomycin
  • Used for certain gram-positive infections that are resistant to other antibiotics
  • Parenterally for systemic infections (poor GI absorption)
  • Inhaled for treatment of lung infections (tobramycin inhalation)
NEOMYCIN — The Exception (Given ORALLY):
  • Given orally to decontaminate the GI tract before surgical procedures
  • Also used as an enema for GI decontamination
  • Used to treat hepatic encephalopathy (kills bacteria in the gut that produce ammonia → reduces ammonia levels → helps brain function)
  • Poorly absorbed orally so it stays in the GI tract where it works locally

Adverse Effects — THE BIG TWO

⚠️ THE TWO MOST SIGNIFICANT TOXICITIES:

1. NEPHROTOXICITY (Renal Damage)
• Aminoglycosides are directly toxic to kidney tubular cells
• Monitor BUN, creatinine, urinary output
• Risk increases with dehydration, concurrent nephrotoxic drugs
• This is why we monitor TROUGH levels — to make sure kidneys are clearing the drug

2. OTOTOXICITY (8th Cranial Nerve Damage)
• Auditory impairment — hearing loss, tinnitus
• Vestibular impairment — vertigo, dizziness, loss of balance
• Affects the EIGHTH cranial nerve (CN VIII = vestibulocochlear nerve)
• May be irreversible!
• Monitor: ask patient about hearing changes, ringing in ears, dizziness

⇒ Must monitor DRUG LEVELS to prevent BOTH toxicities!
Other Adverse Effects
  • Headache
  • Paresthesia (numbness/tingling)
  • Fever
  • Superinfections
  • Vertigo
  • Skin rash
  • Dizziness
Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC)
KEY CONCEPT

The MIC is the lowest concentration of an antibiotic that prevents visible growth of a bacteria. For aminoglycosides, the serum drug level needs to be at least 8 times HIGHER than the MIC to be effective. This is why blood levels are so critical.

Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (TDM)

📌 WHY TDM? Aminoglycosides have a narrow therapeutic index — the difference between a therapeutic dose and a toxic dose is very small. Too low = infection isn't treated. Too high = kidney damage and hearing loss. We must measure blood levels to stay in the safe zone.
Serum Levels — Peak and Trough:

📈 PEAK Level

What: The HIGHEST drug level in the blood

When drawn: 30 min after IV infusion completes (or 1 hr after IM)

Why: Ensures the drug concentration is HIGH ENOUGH to kill the bacteria effectively

Tells us: Is the dose adequate for efficacy?

Used for: Once-daily dosing regimens

📉 TROUGH Level

What: The LOWEST drug level in the blood

When drawn: Just BEFORE the next dose (within 30 min of next scheduled dose)

Why: Ensures adequate renal clearance of the drug — the kidneys have cleared enough drug to avoid accumulation and TOXICITY

Tells us: Is the drug being cleared safely? Are the kidneys working?

Killing Patterns

Two Types of Bacterial Killing — Know the Difference:

⏱️ Time-Dependent Killing

Effectiveness depends on HOW LONG the drug level stays above the MIC

The longer the drug stays above MIC = more bacteria killed

Goal: keep drug above MIC for as long as possible

Example: Beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins) — given multiple times per day

📊 Concentration-Dependent Killing

Effectiveness depends on HOW HIGH the peak level gets above the MIC

The higher the peak above MIC = more bacteria killed

Goal: achieve highest possible peak

Example: AMINOGLYCOSIDES — this is why once-daily high-dose regimens work for aminoglycosides

Aminoglycosides use CONCENTRATION-DEPENDENT killing — this means getting a high peak level is what makes them effective. The serum level should be at least 8× the MIC.

Postantibiotic Effect

Aminoglycosides continue to suppress bacterial growth even AFTER the drug level drops below the MIC. This "postantibiotic effect" is one reason once-daily dosing works — the bacteria are still being suppressed between doses.

Drug Interactions

Aminoglycosides interact with many drugs that are also nephrotoxic or ototoxic. Concurrent use increases risk of toxicity. Examples include: other aminoglycosides, vancomycin, loop diuretics (furosemide), amphotericin B, NSAIDs.

🧠 Fluoroquinolone Dangers
"Floxacins SNAP Tendons — especially Achilles!"
Ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin → FDA black box: tendon rupture (especially Achilles), tendinitis, peripheral neuropathy. Risk ↑ in: elderly, concurrent steroids, kidney transplant. If patient reports tendon pain → STOP the drug immediately.

🧠 Vancomycin — "Red Man, Bad Ears, Bad Kidneys"
🎯 TEACHER FOCUS — Red Man Syndrome: Antibiotic Given Too Fast!
  • Drug: VANCOMYCIN — caused by infusing too quickly
  • What happens: Rapid infusion triggers massive histamine release (NOT a true allergy)
  • Signs: Flushing/redness of face, neck, and upper body ("Red Man"), HYPOTENSION (not hypertension — the BP DROPS), pruritus, rash, tachycardia

Red Man Syndrome — Vancomycin Infused Too FAST = NOT a True Allergy!
⚠️ RED MAN SYNDROME — Critical Distinction:

What it is: Histamine release caused by infusing vancomycin TOO FAST. NOT a true allergic/anaphylactic reaction.

Signs: Flushing/redness of face, neck, and upper chest ("red man"), pruritus, HYPOTENSION (not hypertension — blood pressure DROPS from histamine-mediated vasodilation), tachycardia, possible rash.

Cause: Rapid infusion → massive histamine release from mast cells → vasodilation → ↓BP.

Prevention: Infuse over at least 60 MINUTES (1 gram per hour minimum). Slower for larger doses.

Treatment if it occurs: STOP the infusion → give diphenhydramine (Benadryl) → wait for symptoms to resolve → RESTART at a SLOWER rate. Do NOT discontinue vancomycin permanently — just slow it down.

🧠 KEY: This is NOT an allergy — patient can still receive vancomycin. It's a rate-related reaction. True vancomycin allergy would involve anaphylaxis even at slow rates.

  • Notify provider
  • Give diphenhydramine (Benadryl) as ordered
  • Monitor VS until stable

Physiological Process — Gate Theory Deep Dive

Gate Theory of Pain — Complete Understanding:

The most widely accepted theory of pain transmission. Understanding it explains WHY our interventions work.

The Gate Concept
  • The "gate" is located at the dorsal horn of the spinal cord
  • It regulates whether pain impulses reach the brain
  • Gate OPEN = pain impulses travel to brain = pain is perceived
  • Gate CLOSED = pain impulses are blocked = no pain perception
  • Both peripheral and descending (brain-to-spinal cord) mechanisms can open or close the gate

Tissue injury releases: Bradykinin, Histamine, Potassium, Prostaglandins, Serotonin → These stimulate nerve endings, starting the pain process
↓
2
Nerve impulses enter spinal cord via C fibers → travel up to brain. Entry point = dorsal horn ("the gate"). Gate regulates flow of sensory impulses to brain.
↓
3
If gate is OPEN: Impulses reach brain → pain is perceived. If gate is CLOSED: No impulses to brain → no pain perception.
Closing the Gate (Endogenous Pain Control)
  • Body produces endogenous neurotransmitters: Enkephalins and Endorphins
  • These bind to opioid receptors and inhibit transmission of pain by closing the gate
  • Rubbing a painful area / massage / liniment stimulates large sensory fibers → closes gate → reduces pain sensation
  • Many pain management strategies aim at altering this gate system
4 Steps of Pain Processing
  1. Transduction: Tissue injury & inflammation activate nociceptors
  2. Transmission: C fibers transmit impulse to spinal cord → 2nd order neurons → brain
  3. Perception: Pain is perceived and responses are activated
  4. Modulation: Descending pathways modify/inhibit pain (endorphins, enkephalins)

WHEN TO GIVE NALOXONE (NARCAN)
🎯 Naloxone (Narcan) — When, Why, and How:
  • Give when: RR <10-12/min AND patient is difficult to arouse (significant CNS/respiratory depression from opioids)
  • What it does: Opioid ANTAGONIST — kicks opioids off the receptors → immediately reverses respiratory depression, sedation, hypotension
  • Routes: IV (fastest), IM, SubQ, intranasal (Narcan nasal spray — now OTC)
  • Onset: IV = 1-2 minutes. IM/SubQ = 2-5 minutes
  • ⚠️ Duration: Narcan lasts only 30-90 minutes — but the opioid may last LONGER → patient can crash again after Narcan wears off ("re-narcotization"). Must monitor continuously after giving Narcan!
  • ⚠️ Side effect: Can precipitate acute opioid WITHDRAWAL (pain returns, N/V, tachycardia, sweating, agitation). Titrate slowly — give just enough to restore breathing, NOT to fully wake patient up.
  • Not scheduled — now available OTC without prescription
Your post-op patient received morphine 4mg IV 30 minutes ago. You check on them and find: RR 6, SpO2 84%, pinpoint pupils, difficult to arouse with sternal rub.
What are your PRIORITY nursing actions in order?
1. STOP any opioid infusion. 2. Stimulate patient (call name loudly, sternal rub). 3. Call for help/Rapid Response. 4. Administer naloxone (Narcan) IV as ordered — titrate to RR ≥12. 5. Support airway (jaw thrust, bag-valve mask if needed, O2). 6. Monitor continuously — Narcan wears off in 30-90 min, morphine lasts longer → may need repeat doses. 7. Document everything. The triad of opioid overdose: pinpoint pupils + respiratory depression + altered LOC.
A patient with chronic pain is on hydromorphone (Dilaudid) 2mg IV q4h. Before giving the next dose, you assess: RR 14, alert, pain 8/10. Do you give it?
Yes or no — and why?
YES — give it. RR is 14 (above the hold threshold of 10-12), patient is alert, and they're in significant pain (8/10). Holding pain medication when the patient is safe leads to unrelieved pain → complications. Remember: we hold for RR <10-12 and/or significant sedation, NOT for the pain score itself.

Synthetic drugs that bind to opiate receptors to relieve pain. Main use: moderate to severe pain. Also used for cough suppression, treatment of diarrhea, and balanced anesthesia.

Tolerance, Dependence & Addiction

Know ALL THREE — they are different:
TermDefinitionKey Point
Opioid ToleranceCommon physiologic result of chronic opioid treatment; larger dose required for same analgesiaDevelops in 3-7 days. Expected — NOT addiction.
Physical DependencePhysiologic adaptation of body to opioid presenceExpected with long-term use. Withdrawal occurs if stopped abruptly. NOT addiction.
Psychologic Dependence (Addiction)Compulsive drug use, continued craving, using for effects OTHER than pain reliefThis IS addiction. Different from tolerance/physical dependence.

Tolerance + physical dependence are EXPECTED with chronic use ≠ addiction!

Opioid Ceiling Effect
TESTABLE

Drug reaches a maximum analgesic effect — analgesia does NOT improve even with higher doses. Drugs with ceiling effect: Pentazocine, Nalbuphine (agonist-antagonists), Codeine

Equianalgesia — What Every Nurse Needs to Know

What is Equianalgesia?

The ability to provide equivalent pain relief by calculating dosages of different drugs or routes that provide comparable analgesia. This is essential when switching between opioids or changing routes of administration.

🧠 WHY Nurses Need to Know This:
• Patient is on IV morphine in the hospital → going home on oral oxycodone. How much oxycodone gives the same relief?
• Patient is allergic to morphine → need to switch to hydromorphone. What's the equivalent dose?
• Patient on fentanyl patch → admitted to hospital for surgery → need IV opioid. What dose?
• Getting the conversion WRONG = undertreating pain (too low) or overdosing/killing a patient (too high).

🧠 Equianalgesia Examples — Clinical Scenarios
  • Example 1: Patient receives morphine 10 mg IV followed by hydromorphone 1 mg IV 1 hour later. Total morphine equivalents = 10 + (1 × 7) = 17 mg morphine equivalent.
  • Example 2: Patient is being discharged from IV morphine 4 mg q4h. Need to convert to oral morphine. IV:PO ratio for morphine is 1:3. So 4 mg IV × 3 = 12 mg oral morphine q4h (or ~15 mg to round to available tablet sizes).
  • Example 3: Patient is on oral oxycodone 10 mg. Is that a lot? Oxycodone 20 mg PO = Morphine 30 mg PO. So oxycodone 10 mg PO = ~15 mg morphine PO. That's a moderate dose.
  • When switching opioids: Always reduce the calculated equianalgesic dose by 25-50% to account for incomplete cross-tolerance → then titrate up as needed. This prevents overdose.
💡 Nursing Safety Points for Equianalgesia:
• Always double-check calculations — a decimal point error with fentanyl or hydromorphone can be fatal
• Hydromorphone 1 mg ≠ morphine 1 mg — hydromorphone is 7× stronger! This is a common fatal error
• Oral doses are HIGHER than IV doses for the same drug (first-pass metabolism). Morphine 10 mg IV = morphine 30 mg PO
• Methadone conversions are NOT linear — they require specialist guidance
• When in doubt, START LOW and TITRATE UP — it's safer to underdose slightly and reassess than to overdose

Withdrawal Manifestations

Anxiety, irritability, chills/hot flashes, joint pain, lacrimation (tearing), rhinorrhea (runny nose), diaphoresis, N/V, abdominal cramps, diarrhea, confusion

Naloxone (Narcan):

  • Drug of choice for reversal of opioid-induced respiratory depression
  • Now available OTC / used by first responders
  • If naloxone does NOT reverse symptoms → condition may not be opioid-related
  • Regardless of withdrawal symptoms, if severe respiratory depression → GIVE naloxone

Naltrexone (ReVia): Oral form. Used for alcohol AND opioid addiction maintenance.

Opioid Interactions

Alcohol, antihistamines, barbiturates, benzodiazepines, MAOIs, others — ALL enhance CNS depression

Acetaminophen Adverse Effects:

When to Give Narcan (Naloxone)

Naloxone (Narcan) — Know WHEN, WHY, and HOW!
⚠️ NALOXONE (Narcan) — The Opioid Reversal Agent:

WHEN to give:
  • Respiratory rate <10-12/min (some protocols say <8)
  • Unresponsive or minimally responsive to stimulation
  • Pinpoint pupils + respiratory depression + sedation = classic opioid overdose triad
  • SpO₂ dropping despite supplemental O₂
  • Cyanosis (blue lips/nailbeds)
  • Known or suspected opioid ingestion with altered consciousness

HOW it works: Naloxone is a pure opioid ANTAGONIST — it kicks opioids off the mu receptors → instantly reverses respiratory depression, sedation, and hypotension.

Routes: IV (fastest), IM, SubQ, intranasal (Narcan nasal spray — now OTC)

⚠️ CRITICAL WARNINGS:
  • Short duration — naloxone wears off in 20-60 min, but opioids last longer → patient can re-sedate → MUST monitor closely for re-sedation, may need repeat doses
  • Precipitates withdrawal in opioid-dependent patients → severe pain, agitation, N/V, diaphoresis, tachycardia. Titrate to respiratory function, NOT consciousness.
  • Titrate carefully: Give small doses (0.04-0.4mg) to restore breathing WITHOUT completely reversing analgesia. Goal = RR ≥12, NOT wide awake in pain.

  • 4. Administer naloxone per protocol (usually 0.4mg IV, may give 0.04mg increments in opioid-dependent patients)

NSAIDs — Deep Dive

How NSAIDs Work — MOA
  • Inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes → block prostaglandin synthesis
  • Prostaglandins cause: pain, inflammation, fever, AND protect the stomach lining, maintain renal blood flow, and support platelet aggregation
  • COX-1 = "housekeeping" enzyme: protects GI lining, maintains renal perfusion, supports platelet function (always present)
  • COX-2 = "inflammatory" enzyme: produced at sites of injury/inflammation → causes pain and fever
  • Non-selective NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, ketorolac) block BOTH COX-1 AND COX-2 → pain relief BUT lose protective functions → GI/renal/bleeding risks
  • COX-2 selective (celecoxib/Celebrex) blocks mainly COX-2 → less GI damage but still renal and CV risks
🧠 DISEASE PROCESS THINKING — Why NSAIDs Cause Harm:
When you block COX-1 (the housekeeping enzyme), you lose ALL its protective functions simultaneously. Think of it as disabling a security system to stop an alarm — you also lose the cameras, door locks, and motion sensors. Every NSAID complication traces back to this one mechanism.

NSAID Complication #1: GI Damage

⚠️ GI COMPLICATIONS — From Heartburn to Fatal Hemorrhage:

Mechanism: COX-1 normally produces prostaglandins that maintain the gastric mucosal barrier (the protective lining of the stomach). NSAIDs block COX-1 → prostaglandins ↓ → gastric mucosal barrier breaks down → stomach acid attacks unprotected tissue.

🧠 Disease Process Progression:
Dyspepsia/heartburn → gastritis → erosion → gastric/duodenal ulcer → GI bleeding → perforation → peritonitis → sepsis → death

Signs/Symptoms to Monitor:
• Epigastric pain, heartburn, nausea
• Coffee-ground emesis (upper GI bleed)
• Black tarry stools (melena = digested blood)
• Frank bloody stool (hematochezia = lower or massive upper GI bleed)
• Hemoglobin/hematocrit drop, tachycardia, hypotension (hemorrhage)

Risk Factors for GI Complications:
• Age >60, history of GI ulcers/bleeding, concurrent anticoagulants (warfarin) or corticosteroids, high doses, prolonged use, alcohol use, H. pylori infection

Prevention: Take with food/milk. Consider PPI (proton pump inhibitor) like omeprazole for protection. Use lowest effective dose for shortest time. Use COX-2 selective (celecoxib) if high GI risk.

NSAID Complication #2: RENAL DAMAGE

⚠️ NEPHROTOXICITY — Especially KETOROLAC (Toradol):

Mechanism: COX-1 prostaglandins maintain renal blood flow by keeping the afferent arteriole of the glomerulus DILATED. NSAIDs block this → afferent arteriole constricts → ↓ blood flow to kidneys → ↓ GFR → renal ischemia → acute kidney injury (AKI).

🧠 Disease Process Thinking — Ketorolac (Toradol) is TERRIBLE for Kidneys:
• Toradol is one of the most potent NSAIDs (comparable to morphine for pain relief)
• Because it's so potent, it hits COX-1 HARD → massive reduction in renal prostaglandins
• This is why Toradol has a MAXIMUM of 5 DAYS use — beyond that, risk of renal failure skyrockets
• Even within 5 days, patients with ANY renal risk factors can develop AKI

NSAID Complication #3: CARDIOVASCULAR RISK

⚠️ BLACK BOX WARNING — Increased Risk of MI and Stroke:

Mechanism: NSAIDs (especially COX-2 selective but ALL NSAIDs) shift the balance between thromboxane (promotes clotting/vasoconstriction) and prostacyclin (prevents clotting/vasodilation). Result: prothrombotic state → increased risk of blood clots → MI and stroke.

🧠 Disease Process Thinking:
• Prostacyclin (made by COX-2 in blood vessels) normally prevents clots and keeps vessels dilated
• NSAIDs block prostacyclin production → blood becomes "stickier" → more likely to form clots
• Thromboxane (made by COX-1 in platelets) promotes clotting and is less affected by COX-2 selective drugs
• Result: Unopposed thromboxane = clot formation → MI or stroke

FDA Black Box Warning: ALL NSAIDs increase the risk of serious cardiovascular thrombotic events, MI, and stroke. Risk increases with duration of use and pre-existing CV disease. Contraindicated for perioperative pain in CABG surgery (coronary artery bypass).

Highest risk: Patients with existing heart disease, hypertension, history of MI/stroke, peripheral vascular disease, multiple CV risk factors.

NSAID Complication #4: Bleeding / Platelet Effects

Altered Hemostasis
  • Mechanism: COX-1 in platelets produces thromboxane A₂ → promotes platelet aggregation. NSAIDs block this → ↓ platelet aggregation → ↑ bleeding time
  • Aspirin: Irreversibly inhibits COX in platelets for the LIFETIME of the platelet (7-10 days) — this is why aspirin is used for cardiac protection but also why surgery is delayed after aspirin use
  • Other NSAIDs: Reversible inhibition — effect lasts only while drug is in the system
  • 🧠 Clinical connection: Patient on warfarin (anticoagulant) + NSAID = dramatically increased bleeding risk (GI hemorrhage, surgical bleeding)
  • Monitor: bruising, bleeding gums, blood in stool/urine, prolonged bleeding from cuts

What Is Tuberculosis?

TB — The Disease
  • Caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB)
  • TB is an aerobic bacillus (rod-shaped bacterium that needs oxygen to grow)
  • Most commonly characterized by granulomas in the lungs: nodular accumulations of inflammatory cells (macrophages, lymphocytes) that are walled off with clear boundaries and have a cheesy/caseated center
🧠 DISEASE PROCESS — How TB Works in the Body:

Step 1 — Exposure: Person inhales droplets containing MTB → bacilli enter the alveoli of the lungs.

Step 2 — Immune Response: Alveolar macrophages engulf the bacteria → but MTB is uniquely resistant and can SURVIVE inside macrophages. The immune system calls in more help → forms a granuloma (walled-off collection of immune cells surrounding the bacteria).

Step 3 — Containment or Disease:
  • Healthy immune system: Granuloma walls off the bacteria → bacteria become DORMANT inside the calcified/fibrous tissue → this is Latent TB Infection (LTBI). Patient tests positive for exposure but is NOT infectious and NOT sick.
  • Weakened immune system: Cannot contain the bacteria → granuloma breaks down → bacteria multiply → Active TB Disease. Patient IS infectious and IS sick.

Step 4 — Spread: If uncontained, MTB spreads from the lungs via blood and lymphatic systems to other organs.
Common Infection Sites:
SiteDetails
🫁 LungsPRIMARY site — pulmonary TB is by far the most common. This is where initial infection almost always occurs.
🧠 BrainCerebral cortex — TB meningitis is the most dangerous extrapulmonary form. High mortality.
🦴 BoneGrowing ends of bones (epiphyseal plates). Pott's disease = TB of the spine.
LiverHepatic TB — can mimic hepatitis.
KidneyRenal TB — can cause sterile pyuria (WBCs in urine but negative culture on standard media).
GU TractGenitourinary TB — can cause infertility.

🧠 Think: TB = primarily LUNGS, but can go ANYWHERE via blood/lymph. The lung is the entry point; everything else is dissemination.

Growth & Dormancy

TB Growth Pattern — Why Treatment is So Long:
  • MTB is a VERY SLOW-growing organism — divides every 15-20 hours (most bacteria divide every 20-30 MINUTES)
  • This slow growth makes TB much more difficult to treat than most bacterial infections
  • Antibiotics work best on actively dividing bacteria — dormant bacteria are essentially "hiding" from drugs
  • This is why treatment lasts 6-12 months — you must keep drugs on board long enough to catch bacteria when they come out of dormancy and start dividing
Latent TB vs. Active TB — Critical Distinction:

Latent TB Infection (LTBI)

Bacteria are DORMANT, walled off in granulomas

Positive skin test or blood test

Normal chest X-ray

Negative sputum cultures

NOT infectious

NOT sick (no symptoms)

May need preventive therapy (INH for 6-9 months)

Active TB Disease

Bacteria are MULTIPLYING, destroying tissue

Positive skin test or blood test

Abnormal chest X-ray (infiltrates, cavities)

Positive sputum cultures

INFECTIOUS — can spread to others

SYMPTOMATIC: cough, night sweats, weight loss, fever, hemoptysis, fatigue

Requires multi-drug therapy for 6-12 months

🧠 Why Airborne Precautions (Not Just Droplet)?

Droplet precautions are for organisms that travel in LARGE droplets that fall to the ground within 3-6 feet (flu, meningitis). Airborne precautions are for organisms in TINY droplet nuclei that float in the air for hours and travel on air currents (TB, measles, varicella). TB requires airborne because the particles are so small they remain suspended — you can be infected from across the room or even in a different room connected by ventilation.

When Can Precautions Be Discontinued?
  • Patient is on effective anti-TB drug therapy for at least 2 weeks
  • 3 consecutive negative AFB sputum smears (collected on different days, morning specimens)
  • Clinical improvement (decreased cough, afebrile)
  • 🧠 Key: Major effect of drug therapy = reduction of cough → reduction of infectiousness = normally within 2 weeks if TB strain is drug-sensitive

Mantoux Test (TST / PPD)

How It Works
  • Purified Protein Derivative (PPD) injected intradermally (into the skin, NOT subcutaneous)
  • Dose: 5 tuberculin units (0.1 mL)
  • Read in 48-72 hours
  • Measure the INDURATION (the hard, raised bump) — NOT the redness (erythema)
  • Positive result = delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction = patient has been EXPOSED to TB (does NOT mean active disease)
💡 INDURATION vs. ERYTHEMA: Only measure the INDURATION (the firm, raised area you can feel). Redness alone is NOT a positive result. Use a ballpoint pen method or palpation to define the edges of induration. Measure in MILLIMETERS across the forearm (transversely).

🩸 QuantiFERON-TB Gold

Blood test (single draw)

Measures interferon-gamma released by T-cells when exposed to TB antigens in vitro

Advantages:

  • Single visit (no 48-72 hr return)
  • NOT affected by BCG vaccination (no false positives)
  • Less subjective (lab result vs. measuring induration)
  • Results in 24 hours

💉 Mantoux (TST/PPD)

Intradermal injection

Measures delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction (induration) at injection site

Advantages:

  • Cheap and widely available
  • Long track record
  • No lab needed

Limitations: Requires 2 visits (inject + read); BCG vaccination causes FALSE POSITIVES; reader variability; boosted response on repeat testing

Epidemiologist's Bathtub — Good to Know

🛁 Epidemiologist's Bathtub Analogy

Imagine the TB epidemic as a bathtub. The faucet represents new cases (incidence) and the drain represents people recovering/dying (leaving the disease pool). The water level = prevalence (total cases at any point).

  • Faucet (new cases): Transmission rate, immigration from high-prevalence areas, HIV coinfection creating new active cases from latent infections, reactivation in immunosuppressed
  • Drain (cases leaving): Successful treatment completion, death, natural immune containment
  • If faucet > drain: Prevalence rises (epidemic grows). This happened in 1985 with HIV → TB resurgence
  • If drain > faucet: Prevalence falls (we're winning). This happened with intensified public health efforts post-1992
  • The MDR-TB problem: Like partially clogging the drain — treatment failures stay in the tub AND the faucet keeps running because resistant strains spread

🧠 Bottom line: To empty the bathtub, you must: reduce transmission (airborne precautions, contact tracing), treat infections effectively (multi-drug therapy, ensure adherence), AND prevent reactivation (treat latent TB, manage HIV).

Multidrug-Resistant TB

MDR-TB vs. XDR-TB:

MDR-TB

Resistant to BOTH of the two best first-line drugs:

Isoniazid (INH) + Rifampin

Growing concern worldwide. Caused by incomplete treatment, nonadherence, inadequate drug regimens.

XDR-TB (Extensively Drug-Resistant)

Resistant to INH + Rifampin PLUS the best second-line medications

Resistant to almost ALL drugs used to treat TB

Special concern for HIV/AIDS patients

Very high mortality rate

Why Does Resistance Develop?
  • Nonadherence — #1 cause. Patients feel better after a few weeks → stop taking medications → surviving bacteria are the ones that were starting to develop resistance → they multiply → MDR-TB
  • Inadequate regimen — treating with too few drugs or wrong drugs
  • Insufficient duration — stopping before full course (6-12 months) completed
  • Interrupted drug supply — common in developing countries
  • 🧠 This is EXACTLY why multiple drugs are used simultaneously — a bacterium that develops resistance to one drug will be killed by the others. This is the same principle as HIV antiretroviral "cocktails" and cancer chemotherapy.
⚠️ History of TB in America:
1950s-1985: TB incidence decreased steadily.
1985: TB began RISING for first time in 20 years — driven by HIV epidemic creating active TB from latent infections.
1992: Peak of resurgence in the US.
Post-1992: Decreased due to intensified public health efforts (screening, DOT, contact tracing).
Current concern: Increasing MDR-TB cases. Nonadherence to prolonged treatment remains a growing concern.
Global: TB infects one-third of the world's population.

BCG (Bacille Calmette-Guérin):
  • Vaccine derived from an inactivated strain of Mycobacterium bovis
  • Used in much of the world to vaccinate young children against TB
  • NOT used in the United States

What is a "Slow Acetylator"?
  • INH is metabolized in the liver through a process called acetylation
  • Some patients are genetically "slow acetylators" — their livers lack sufficient enzymes to break down INH at a normal rate

  • Nursing action: Dosage of INH may need to be LOWERED to prevent accumulation and toxicity

Why MULTIPLE Drugs Simultaneously?
  • TB has a natural mutation rate → in any large bacterial population, some bacteria are already resistant to any single drug
  • If you use ONE drug → kills susceptible bacteria → resistant ones survive → multiply → now you have drug-resistant TB
  • Multiple drugs = multiple kill mechanisms → a bacterium resistant to Drug A will be killed by Drug B, C, or D
  • The probability of a bacterium being simultaneously resistant to 4 drugs is astronomically low
  • Same principle as HIV antiretroviral cocktails
  • Start multi-drug therapy BEFORE susceptibility results are known → adjust after results come back

  • Sufficient duration of treatment
  • Adherence to drug regimen (biggest problem)
  • Selection of an effective drug combination

Nonadherence — The Cycle of Disaster:
  • Patient starts 4-drug therapy → feels better in 2 weeks (cough decreases) → thinks they're cured → stops taking medications
  • The bacteria that were killed first = the drug-sensitive ones (easy to kill)
  • The bacteria still alive after partial treatment = the ones that were developing resistance
  • Without drug pressure, these resistant bacteria multiply freely → now the patient has MDR-TB
  • Patient becomes symptomatic again → now needs longer, more toxic treatment AND has been spreading resistant TB
  • 🧠 This is why DOT exists and why patient education about completing the FULL course is the single most important nursing intervention for TB.
⚠️ DOT — Directly Observed Therapy:
Because nonadherence is the #1 threat to TB treatment success (and the main cause of MDR-TB), the gold standard is DOT — a healthcare worker WATCHES the patient take every single dose. This is a public health measure. TB is one of the few diseases where treatment is a public health mandate, not just individual choice, because untreated TB endangers the entire community.
What If Treatment Isn't Working?
  • If no improvement within 2 weeks → suspect drug resistance
  • Do NOT assume the patient is nonadherent — first evaluate for resistance
  • Adjust regimen based on susceptibility testing results
  • In extreme cases (resistant to almost all drugs, patient allergic to the one drug that works): patient may receive the drug they're allergic to WITH supportive care (antipyretics, antihistamines, corticosteroids) because the urgency of treating a fatal infection outweighs the contraindication

Gout — Disease Process

What Is Gout?

A condition that results from inappropriate uric acid metabolism leading to hyperuricemia (elevated uric acid in blood). Uric acid crystals deposit in tissues and joints → intense inflammatory pain.

🧠 DISEASE PROCESS — How Gout Works:

Step 1 — Uric Acid Buildup: Uric acid is the end product of purine metabolism. Normally, the kidneys excrete excess uric acid. In gout, there is either:
• Underexcretion of uric acid by kidneys (most common — ~90% of cases)
• Overproduction of uric acid (from excess purine breakdown)

Step 2 — Hyperuricemia: When serum uric acid exceeds ~6.8 mg/dL, it reaches its saturation point in blood → can no longer stay dissolved.

Step 3 — Crystal Formation: Monosodium urate (MSU) crystals form and deposit in joints, soft tissues, and kidneys. Crystals prefer cooler body parts → big toe (MTP joint), ankles, knees, fingers, elbows.

Step 4 — Inflammatory Response: WBCs (neutrophils) try to engulf crystals → release inflammatory mediators → intense, excruciating pain, redness, swelling, warmth in the affected joint.

Step 5 — Chronic Damage (if untreated): Repeated attacks → tophi (visible uric acid crystal deposits under skin), chronic joint destruction, kidney stones (uric acid stones), renal damage.

Classic Gout Presentation
  • Podagra: Acute gout attack in the first MTP joint (big toe) — most classic presentation
  • Sudden onset — often wakes patient from sleep
  • Joint is red, hot, swollen, exquisitely tender (even bedsheets touching it causes pain)
  • More common in: men, postmenopausal women, obesity, alcohol use, high-purine diet, kidney disease, certain medications (thiazide diuretics)
🎯 TEACHER FOCUS — Probenecid & The Kidneys
  • Probenecid NEEDS good kidneys to work! It works by blocking uric acid reabsorption in the kidney tubules → kidneys excrete more uric acid
  • If kidneys are impaired (CrCl <50) → probenecid is USELESS because the kidneys can't excrete what probenecid is trying to push out
  • Kidney stone risk: More uric acid in urine → concentrated urine → uric acid crystallizes → KIDNEY STONES
  • Prevention: Drink ≥2 liters/day to dilute urine. May alkalinize urine (uric acid dissolves better in alkaline urine)
  • Do NOT combine with aspirin — aspirin blocks probenecid's uricosuric effect

Nursing — Fluids, Diet, GI, & Monitoring

Therapeutic Effect: Uric Acid Levels

How You Know the Drug Is Working:
  • Target serum uric acid: <6.0 mg/dL
  • Below 6.0 → existing crystals dissolve over time → fewer attacks → tophi shrink
  • Above 6.0 → crystals continue to form → more attacks → more joint damage
  • Monitor uric acid levels regularly (baseline, then periodic — monthly initially, then every 3-6 months once stable)
  • Decreased frequency and severity of gout attacks
  • Decreased joint swelling, redness, pain
  • Resolution of tophi over time (can take months to years)
  • ⚠️ Note: When starting allopurinol/febuxostat, uric acid levels DROP → crystals mobilize → can trigger an ACUTE attack ("mobilization flare"). This is why colchicine is often given ALONGSIDE when starting uric acid–lowering therapy.

Fluid Intake — CRITICAL

⚠️ FLUID INTAKE — Required for ALL Antigout Drugs:

Minimum: At least 2 LITERS (8 full glasses) of fluid per day

Why:
  • Antigout drugs (especially uricosurics like probenecid and lesinurad) increase uric acid excretion through the kidneys
  • More uric acid in urine → if urine is concentrated → uric acid can crystallize in the renal tubules and ureters → KIDNEY STONES
  • Adequate fluid dilutes the urine → keeps uric acid dissolved → prevents stone formation
  • Also helps maintain renal function — kidneys need adequate hydration to excrete drugs and uric acid

Nursing Actions:
  • Educate: drink at least 2 liters of water daily
  • Monitor I&Os if hospitalized
  • Encourage water — avoid excessive caffeine/alcohol (both dehydrate)
  • May alkalinize urine (sodium bicarbonate) to increase uric acid solubility — uric acid is more soluble in alkaline urine

Complete Nursing Monitoring

🩺 Nursing Assessment & Monitoring — ALL Antigout Drugs
  • Baseline labs: Serum uric acid, renal function (BUN/Cr, eGFR), LFTs, CBC
  • Ongoing: Serum uric acid levels (target <6.0 mg/dL), renal function, LFTs
  • Monitor I&Os — ensure adequate fluid intake (≥2L/day)
  • Assess joints: Swelling, redness, pain, range of motion, presence of tophi
  • Allopurinol specifically: Assess skin at EVERY visit — report ANY rash immediately (risk of SJS/TEN which can be FATAL)
  • Febuxostat specifically: Monitor for cardiovascular symptoms (chest pain, SOB, sudden weakness)
  • Colchicine specifically: Monitor for GI toxicity (N/V/D = dose-limiting). Monitor CBC (leukopenia risk).
  • Probenecid specifically: Avoid aspirin/salicylates. Verify adequate renal function. Monitor for kidney stones (flank pain, hematuria).
  • Lesinurad specifically: Monitor serum creatinine. NEVER give without concurrent xanthine oxidase inhibitor.
🩺 Patient Education
  • Drink at least 2 liters of fluid daily — prevents kidney stones
  • Avoid alcohol — especially beer
  • Avoid high-purine foods — organ meats, sardines, anchovies, shellfish
  • Do NOT take aspirin if on probenecid — use acetaminophen for minor pain
  • Take medications with food to reduce GI upset (most antigout drugs)
  • Report immediately: Any skin rash (especially allopurinol), vision changes, severe GI symptoms, signs of infection (fever), flank pain/blood in urine (kidney stones), chest pain (febuxostat)
  • Therapy effects may take weeks to months — don't expect immediate results from uric acid–lowering drugs
  • A gout flare when starting new medication is NORMAL (mobilization flare) — don't stop the medication
  • Therapeutic effects: ↓ uric acid levels, ↓ frequency of attacks, ↓ joint swelling/pain
Misoprostol (Cytotec) — GI Protection
  • Synthetic prostaglandin E1 analogue
  • Prevents GI bleeding from NSAIDs
  • Inhibits gastric acid secretion + has cytoprotective component (protects stomach lining)
  • Given alongside NSAIDs in high-risk patients
  • ⚠️ Contraindicated in PREGNANCY — causes uterine contractions → miscarriage/premature labor

Ch 38 — Antibiotics Pt 1

Culture & Sensitivity BEFORE First Dose!
ALWAYS collect specimens (blood cultures, urine C&S, wound culture) BEFORE starting antibiotics. Antibiotics kill bacteria → culture returns falsely negative → you lose the chance to identify the bug. "SWAB then STAB."
Empiric vs. Prophylactic vs. Definitive Therapy:
• Empiric = best-guess treatment started before C&S results return
• Prophylactic = antibiotics to PREVENT infection (pre-surgical cefazolin, dental amoxicillin for heart valves)
• Definitive = targeted treatment after C&S identifies the organism
Superinfections = Antibiotics Causing NEW Infections:
• C. diff: Watery diarrhea 10-15x/day, foul smell → STOP antibiotic → oral vancomycin → contact precautions → clean with BLEACH
• Oral thrush: White patches → nystatin swish-and-swallow
• Vaginal yeast: Itching/discharge → fluconazole
Penicillin Key Points:
• Amoxicillin = #1 prescribed for kids (otitis media)
• Augmentin = amoxicillin with bodyguard (clavulanate)
• Pen allergy = clarify RASH vs ANAPHYLAXIS before avoiding all beta-lactams
• Nafcillin/oxacillin = for MSSA (but NOT MRSA)
Cephalosporin Generations:
Higher generation = better Gram-negative, worse Gram-positive. Alcohol + cephalosporins = disulfiram reaction (N/V/flushing). Cross-reactivity with penicillin allergy (1-10%).
Tetracycline Rules — "No MILK, No SUN, No BABIES, No KIDS under 8":
• Dairy/calcium/antacids/iron chelate the drug → won't absorb
• Photosensitivity → sunburn
• Pregnancy → permanent tooth staining in fetus
• Children <8 → permanent yellow teeth
Patient Education for ALL Antibiotics:
Complete the ENTIRE course. Report allergic reactions immediately. Watch for superinfection (new diarrhea, white mouth patches, vaginal itching). No alcohol with metronidazole/cephalosporins. OCs may be less effective.

Ch 39 — Antibiotics Pt 2

MDROs — The Big 4:
• MRSA → treat with vancomycin (IV) or TMP-SMX/doxycycline (PO)
• VRE → treat with linezolid or daptomycin
• ESBL → treat with carbapenems
• CRE → "nightmare bacteria," ~50% mortality, very few options

• Ototoxic (ears: tinnitus, hearing loss, vertigo) — irreversible!
• Nephrotoxic (kidneys: ↑BUN/Cr, ↓urine output)

• Trough drawn right BEFORE next dose. Peak 30 min after IV ends.

• Caused by infusing TOO FAST (not a true allergy!)
• Flushing, redness, HYPOTENSION (not hypertension)
• Prevention: Infuse over ≥60 minutes
• Treatment: STOP infusion → diphenhydramine → restart SLOWER
• Patient CAN still receive vancomycin — just slower
Fluoroquinolones (-floxacins) Black Box:
• Tendon rupture (especially Achilles), tendinitis, peripheral neuropathy
• Risk ↑ with: elderly, steroids, kidney transplant
• Tendon pain → STOP immediately

Ch 10 — Analgesics / Pain

Aspirin — Who NOT to Give It To:
• Children/teens <19 with viral illness → Reye's Syndrome (brain swelling + liver failure → death)
• Active bleeding / bleeding disorders / pre-surgery (irreversibly blocks platelets 7-10 days)
• Peptic ulcer disease / GI bleeding history
• Patients on warfarin (double anticoagulation)
• Gout patients on probenecid (blocks probenecid from working)
• Third trimester pregnancy
Why NSAIDs Over Opioids:
NSAIDs: No addiction, no respiratory depression, no constipation, anti-inflammatory, work at pain source.
Opioids: Respiratory depression (#1 killer), addiction, constipation (no tolerance develops), sedation, withdrawal.
Use WHO Pain Ladder: Non-opioids first → weak opioids → strong opioids. Multimodal = best.
Naloxone (Narcan) — When to Give:
• RR <10-12 + pinpoint pupils + sedation = opioid overdose triad
• Routes: IV (fastest), IM, SubQ, intranasal (now OTC)
• ⚠️ Short duration — wears off in 20-60 min → opioid re-sedation → monitor closely, repeat doses
• Titrate to RR ≥12, NOT wide awake (precipitates withdrawal in dependent patients)
Acetaminophen Max Doses:
• Healthy adult: 3,000 mg/day
• Liver disease/alcohol/elderly: 2,000 mg/day
• Overdose → hepatic necrosis (FATAL). Antidote = acetylcysteine (Mucomyst)
• Watch for hidden acetaminophen in combo products (Percocet, Vicodin, NyQuil)
NSAIDs "GRaB" — GI, Renal, Bleeding + CV:
• GI: Ulcers/bleeding (COX-1 lost) → take with food, PPI
• Renal: AKI (lost renal prostaglandins) → ketorolac MAX 5 DAYS
• Bleeding: ↓platelets (aspirin irreversible, others reversible)
• CV: Black box — ↑MI/stroke risk (all except aspirin)

Ch 44 — Antigout

Gout Therapeutic Target:
Serum uric acid <6.0 mg/dL. Below 6 → crystals dissolve. Above 6 → crystals form. Fluid intake ≥2L/day.
Gout Drug Actions:
• Allopurinol = stops uric acid PRODUCTION (xanthine oxidase inhibitor) → ⚠️ SJS/TEN (stop at first rash!)
• Probenecid = increases uric acid EXCRETION (needs good kidneys!) → aspirin blocks it → kidney stones
• Colchicine = reduces INFLAMMATION (doesn't lower uric acid) → GI toxicity is dose-limiting
• Febuxostat = like allopurinol but ⚠️ FDA black box for ↑CV events
Avoid in Gout: Alcohol (especially beer), high-purine foods (organ meats, sardines), aspirin (blocks probenecid), thiazide diuretics (↓ uric acid excretion).
